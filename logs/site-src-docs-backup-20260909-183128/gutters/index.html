Why color comes first

A gutter that fights the house was picked in a driveway

Gutter color gets specified from a card held at arm's length in a parking lot. That works on a white vinyl house. It falls apart on everything else.

Stock white K-style against the buff-and-red face brick of a 1960s ranch makes the gutter the brightest thing on the elevation. Mill-finish aluminum against a charcoal shingle reads as unfinished. Dark bronze against weathered cedar loses the eave line instead of sharpening it.

So we bring samples to the house and hold them where the gutter will sit: against fascia, trim, roof field and brick, in the light that elevation actually gets. A south-facing Upper Arlington front sees hard afternoon sun; a shaded Dublin rear elevation does not.

Then we decide out loud, with you standing there: match the trim, match the roof, or set the gutter back against the masonry. All three are defensible; picking one by accident is not. It is the method we use on shingles and siding, described on the Matchwork method page.

Nothing gets ordered until you have seen the sample against your own trim and brick.

How the job runs

Measure, decide, form, hang, test

  1. Measure the roof, not the fascia

    Size follows the roof area and pitch feeding each run and how many valleys concentrate into one outlet. We measure the roof and work backwards.

  2. Check what we are hanging it to

    Fascia and sub-fascia carry the whole load. Rotted wood gets repaired first, or the new gutter pulls out over a few freeze-thaw cycles.

  3. Decide color and profile at the house

    Samples against trim, brick and roof field. K-style or half-round, painted aluminum or copper. You see it before it is ordered.

  4. Form on site and hang to pitch

    Runs are formed at your driveway to the measured length, hung into solid wood on a consistent spacing, pitched to the outlet rather than level.

  5. Test with water before we leave

    Every run gets flooded and watched: corners stay dry, the outlet takes the flow, the downspout discharges where we said it would.

What we find

Six gutter failures on a Columbus house

Knowing which one you have is most of the diagnosis.

Failed seam

A sectioned gutter leaks at every joint once the sealant goes: a streak down the fascia, a mineral line on the brick, dead grass at the drip line.

Lost pitch

Hangers pull out of soft fascia and the run sags between them. Water stands, freezes, and finds the low seam. Sight along the eave and you can see it.

Undersized run or outlet

A five-inch gutter under a steep roof with two valleys feeding one outlet overspills in a hard storm even when it is clean and pitched correctly.

Ice at the eave

An ice dam freezes the gutter solid to the fascia, and when it lets go it takes hangers with it. That is an attic problem wearing a gutter costume.

Fascia rot behind the gutter

Hidden until the gutter comes off. Overflow behind the run keeps the wood wet all winter. We open it up and show you before quoting the repair.

Downspout at the foundation

A downspout ending eighteen inches from the wall puts every gallon back into the soil beside your footing. The gutter worked; the drainage did not.

When the failure is really at the eave — ice dam, step flashing, rotted soffit — we say so. Small fixes go through the gutter section of our exterior repairs page; roof-side leaks through roof leak and flashing repair.

Common questions

What homeowners ask about gutters

Can you match a trim color that is no longer standard?

Usually. Painted gutter coil comes in a wide range, and where no factory color lands we match to a sample of your trim. Copper skips the question: it is a finish that changes, not a color you choose.

Should gutters be replaced with the roof?

If they are original to a roof at the end of its life, yes. A tear-off wrecks old gutters anyway, and drip edge, gutter and first course are one system.

Why is there no price list here?

Footage, size, profile, material, fascia condition and downspout work all move the number. We measure yours, then write an itemized proposal. Copper sits above painted aluminum.
