What a replacement includes

Every layer has a job

A shingle is the last thing you see. Everything under it is what keeps water out.

We tear off down to the deck, check it for soft spots, and rebuild the system in order — leak barrier, underlayment, starter course, field shingles, ridge. Nothing skipped, nothing hidden under the new roof.

Ice-and-water shield

Set along eaves, valleys and around penetrations, where ice dams and wind-driven rain do the most damage.

Synthetic underlayment

A tear-resistant layer across the full deck, the backstop under the shingle field.

Starter course & drip edge

Sealed starter shingles and a clean drip edge at every eave and rake, so wind can't get under the first course.

Ridge & ventilation

A balanced ridge vent to move attic air and protect the deck from the inside out.

The full layer-by-layer reasoning, and why the parts you cannot see decide how the roof performs, is on the Columbus roofing hub and the Matchwork Method page.

The part nobody sees

What comes off, and what we check when it does

A tear-off is not demolition. It is the only chance anyone gets to look at the deck before it is covered for decades.

Everything comes off: shingles, underlayment, the old starter course, the old drip edge, and every flashing that is being replaced rather than reused. Where a second layer exists, both come off. Layering over an existing roof hides the deck, keeps old fasteners and old flashing in place, adds dead load the framing was not designed for, and gives the new shingles an uneven surface to lie on. We do not quote it as the good option.

Once the deck is bare we walk it. Soft spots get probed, not eyeballed. Sheathing that is delaminated, water-stained or has lost its fastener grip gets replaced, and every sheet we pull is shown to you or photographed before the new one goes on. Existing fasteners that have backed out get re-driven or replaced. Deck joints and h-clips get checked, because a deck that flexes between rafters cracks shingles from underneath.

Then the flashings. Valley metal, step flashing at sidewalls and dormers, chimney base flashing and counterflashing, pipe boots, vent collars, drip edge and the kickouts at the gutters are all assessed individually and listed on the proposal as replaced or reused, with a reason. A new shingle field over old flashing is the most common way a brand-new roof leaks in its second winter.

  • Every layer removed, including a second roof where one exists.
  • Deck probed for soft spots, not just looked at.
  • Any sheathing pulled is shown to you or photographed first.
  • Fasteners re-driven, deck joints and clips checked.
  • Each flashing listed on the proposal as replaced or reused, with a reason.
  • Gutter interface assessed, because the drip edge and the gutter are one detail.

Where the flashing at a chimney is the real problem, that is a two-trade detail and the masonry half of it is on the chimney page.

How it runs

Measurement, color, proposal

Measure

Aerial measurement gives us exact facets, pitch and waste factor before we quote anything.

Match

We bring physical samples to your house and match them against your brick, siding and trim in daylight.

Propose

A written, itemized proposal — the scope, the materials, the schedule.

Nothing gets ordered until the color has been held against your house in two different lights. A shingle that reads warm brown on a supplier's rack can go plum against a Georgian red brick, or flat gray against a warm limestone, and by the time that is obvious the pallets are on the drive. The matching method is described in full on the Matchwork Method page, and the roofing-specific version, including what happens when only part of a roof is being touched, is on the shingle matching page.

The proposal names the exact product line and color, the underlayment, the ice-and-water coverage and where it runs, the ventilation, every flashing being replaced, what happens to the gutters, and what we are deliberately not doing. You should be able to read it without us standing beside you explaining it.

During the work

The house gets protected, and the site gets left clean

A tear-off is violent. Old shingles come off in sheets, granules go everywhere, and nails land in the lawn whether anyone wants them to or not. How that is managed is the difference between a contractor who finishes a roof and one who finishes a roof and a garden.

Landscaping gets covered before the first shingle comes off. Windows below the work get protected when the tear-off is heavy. Ground protection goes down at the dumpster and along the route the material travels. Magnets are run over the drive, the walk and the lawn at the end of every work day, not only on the last one, because a nail found in a tire six months later is still our nail.

Attic ventilation is dealt with at the same time rather than left as it was. Intake at the eaves gets checked for blockage by insulation, exhaust at the ridge gets balanced against it, and any bathroom or kitchen vent that terminates in the attic instead of through the roof gets corrected. A new shingle field over a hot, damp attic will not perform the way the same field does over a vented one.

  • Landscaping covered, windows below protected, ground protection at the dumpster.
  • Magnetic nail pickup at the end of every work day, not just the last.
  • Intake and exhaust ventilation balanced, and blocked soffits cleared.
  • Any vent terminating in the attic corrected to the exterior.
  • Gutters cleaned out of the tear-off debris before we leave.
  • A final walk with you, and the photographs and paperwork in one place.

Gutters that are at the end of their life, or that are the wrong size for the roof above them, are handled on the same contract and sequenced into the same project rather than a separate mobilization later. That work is on the gutters hub.

Common questions

What homeowners ask

How long does a replacement take?

A single-family architectural shingle replacement typically finishes in one to two days once materials are on site. Complex rooflines take longer. Your proposal states the schedule before you sign.

Can you match new shingles to what's already on my roof?

That's the reason for the name. We bring physical sample boards and compare colorway, shadow blend and granule texture against your existing roof and your home's exterior in the light your house actually sits in.

Why isn't there a price list on this page?

Roof area, pitch, facet count, deck condition and material choice all change the number — sometimes by a wide margin between two homes on the same street. We measure yours, then hand you a fixed, itemized proposal.

Can you shingle over what I have?

We do not recommend it and we will not quote it as the good option. A roof-over hides the deck, keeps the old flashing, adds load, and gives the new shingles nothing flat to lie on. It also disqualifies the roof from extended manufacturer coverage. We tear off to the deck so we can see what we are fastening to.

What if you find rotten decking?

We show it to you or photograph it before anything is covered up, and it is priced as an addition against the sheet count. It is not a surprise line on the final invoice, and it is not something we decide alone once the roof is open.

What happens to my gutters?

A tear-off puts weight and debris on them. They get cleaned out at the end, and any section that is bent, sagging or spiked through the fascia gets noted. Whether they are repaired, re-hung or left alone is on the proposal, not decided on the day.

If what you have is one failed detail rather than a roof at the end of its life, that is a repair and we will scope it as one. Our roofing repairs in Columbus cover leaks, flashing, vents and storm damage, and the diagnostic method behind them is on the roof leak and flashing repair page.
