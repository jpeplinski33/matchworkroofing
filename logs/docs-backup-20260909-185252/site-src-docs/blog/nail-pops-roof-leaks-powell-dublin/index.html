Roof diagnostics

Why a small nail pop can cause a real leak

A nail pop is one of the smallest defects a roof can have, and one of the easiest to miss from the ground. It's also one of the more common reasons a roof that looks fine develops a slow, hard-to-place leak in Powell and Dublin homes.

The basics

What a nail pop actually is

Shingles are fastened to the roof deck with nails driven flush, below the surface of the shingle above them so the nail head sits protected. A nail pop is what happens when one of those nails works its way back out of the deck — sometimes a fraction of an inch, sometimes far enough to lift the shingle above it into a small visible bump. Once a nail has backed out even slightly, it's no longer holding the shingle down the way it should, and it's opened a small path into the roof deck around its own shank.

Why it happens

Why decking moves in the first place

Roof decking — plywood or OSB — isn't a static surface. It expands and contracts with temperature and humidity, and Central Ohio gives it plenty of both to react to: humid summer afternoons, dry winter cold snaps, and everything between. Over enough of those cycles, a smooth-shank nail can work loose from the wood fiber gripping it, the same way any fastener loosens with repeated small movements. A few conditions make it worse:

  • Smooth-shank nails instead of ring-shank, which grip the deck less firmly once the wood around them has flexed a few times.
  • A nail driven at an angle rather than straight down, so it's already bearing sideways load before the deck ever moves.
  • Excess moisture in the attic softening the underside of the deck and weakening its hold on the fastener.
  • A nail set slightly high to begin with, giving the deck's movement less distance to travel before it pushes through.

Placement matters as much as the fastener. A shingle has a nailing zone marked on it, just below the sealant strip, and a nail set outside that zone — too high into the exposed tab, or too low through the sealant — does less holding and more puncturing. High-set nails are also the ones most likely to end up standing proud, because there is less shingle above them to keep the head buried.

How it leaks

How a popped nail turns into a leak

Once a nail head is standing proud of the deck, it's pressing up against the shingle directly above it. Foot traffic, ice, or simple age can eventually let that nail head pierce through the asphalt layer of the shingle covering it. From that point on, rain that lands anywhere near the nail can trace down the exposed shank and into the deck, rather than running off the shingle surface the way it's supposed to. It's a slow leak by nature — usually just a trickle, following the nail rather than pooling — which is exactly why it can go unnoticed for a long time and show up somewhere unexpected, like a stain on a ceiling well away from where the actual nail sits.

A single nail pop, caught early, is a minor repair. Left alone for a season or two, the moisture around it can start softening the deck itself, which turns a small fix into a section of deck replacement. That's the main reason it's worth an occasional look rather than waiting for a ceiling stain to prompt one.

Ice makes it worse in winter. Water in around a shank freezes, expands and pushes the fastener further out; the next thaw lets more in. A popped nail in February on a north slope in Westerville or Upper Arlington does more damage per week than the same nail in August, even though the ceiling stain looks identical.

The repair

What a proper repair looks like

The wrong fix is fast and tempting: hammer the raised nail head back down flat and caulk over it. That can hold for a while, but it doesn't remove the nail from the path of least resistance for water, and a nail that's already loosened once tends to work its way back up again as the deck keeps moving through future seasons. A repair that actually holds means pulling the popped nail out entirely, filling and sealing the hole it leaves, and setting a new fastener — properly seated, in solid decking a few inches away — to hold that section of shingle down going forward. On a full replacement, using ring-shank nails and a correctly calibrated nail gun from the start prevents most of this from happening again.

Two details decide whether that repair still holds. The new fastener has to land in sound decking, not in the softened wood the old one worked loose from, which sometimes means lifting a shingle or two to see what you're fastening into. And the seal has to be the right material in the right amount: enough to close the old hole and bed the shingle down, not so much that it dams water under the course above it. A blob of sealant on top of a shingle usually hides a problem rather than fixing one.

If a popped nail is one of several, or the shingle around it has started to crack and lose granule, the honest answer may be a slope rather than a spot. The roof repairs we get called to covers where that line usually falls, and roof leak and flashing repair explains how a leak gets traced to its actual source instead of its stain.

Other causes

What else lifts a shingle, and why the difference matters

A bump in a shingle course isn't always a nail. Three other things look similar from the yard and are treated differently up close:

  • Wind creasing. A shingle lifted and flexed in straight-line wind develops a horizontal crease across the tab, sometimes with the sealant strip broken. The fasteners are where they belong; the shingle is the part that's damaged.
  • Cupping and curling. Edges turning up or a tab dishing in the middle is aging and attic heat, not a fastener problem, and it shows across a whole slope rather than at one point.
  • A telegraphed fastener. Where a new roof went over an old one, an old nail head can press up through the new shingle years later. That's a different repair, and a different conversation about the deck.

Telling them apart is a short job for someone standing on the roof and a guessing game from a driveway. What a roof inspection looks at is the version of that visit written out.

Spotting one

How to spot a nail pop before it leaks

From the ground, look for small, regularly spaced bumps in a shingle course, especially on slopes that get the most sun and temperature swing. In the attic, a flashlight run along the underside of the deck on a bright day will sometimes show pinpricks of daylight where a nail has pierced all the way through. Neither check takes long, and both are worth doing once a year, particularly on a roof ten years old or more.

Two more things are worth checking while you're there. A dark streak or stain on a ceiling is worth tracing to the roof above it even where the two don't line up, because water travels along decking and rafters before it drops. If the attic feels hot and damp in summer, or the deck's underside looks dark in patches, ventilation and moisture are part of the problem too.
