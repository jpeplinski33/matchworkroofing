Vinyl siding

Insulated vinyl, board and batten, honest limits

Vinyl is a real option on some Columbus houses. We install it properly when it is, and we say so plainly when it is not.

Free estimate How we match siding to a house

What we install

Insulated vinyl, and what the foam actually does

Insulated vinyl is a lap panel with a rigid foam backing on the inside face. The backing does three useful things: it stops the panel telegraphing every wave in the sheathing, which is the ripple you see on a lot of re-sided houses; it stiffens the panel against bumps; and it cuts air movement behind the siding, a modest but real gain on an older Columbus wall.

What the foam does not do is fix rot or make a thin panel behave like cement board. Modern vinyl holds color far better than the product from thirty years ago, and deep shades exist now that used to mean paint.

Style

Board and batten in vinyl

Board and batten is a profile, not a material. Vinyl carries it, and on a simple gabled elevation it can read well, particularly in a deep body color with a contrasting batten.

The catch is the substrate under the profile. Vinyl batten is a shaped panel, so the shadow is shallower than a real batten over a real board, and long runs show unevenness in the wall behind them.

If board and batten is the look you want, board and batten in Hardie, LP and vinyl walks through how each material carries the line over a long wall.

Where it is right

When vinyl is the sensible answer

  • The wall is sound, the wrap can be replaced, and the budget has a ceiling. Vinyl is the lowest cost band of the three materials we install.
  • A rental, a flip or a house you intend to sell soon, where the finish needs to look right rather than last a generation.
  • Soffit, fascia and trim work where vinyl or aluminum soffit panels are the practical match for what is already there. See soffit, fascia and trim repair.
  • A rear or garage elevation that nobody sees from the street, where spending cement-board money buys nothing.

Installation detail that matters most

Vinyl expands and contracts more than any other siding we install. Panels hang on the fastener, they are not pinned tight: the fastener sits centered in the nailing slot, driven to leave a little play, and every cut end and trim intersection keeps its gap. A panel driven tight in October buckles in July and splits in January.

Honest limits

What vinyl cannot do

It cannot be painted to match a repair

Color runs through the panel, so a patch comes from whatever lot is in stock. Painting vinyl is possible but limited, and a painted repair will not disappear into a faded field. If an invisible repair is the goal, matching siding that is no longer made explains what is left.

It cracks in cold

Below freezing, vinyl gets brittle. A branch, a ladder or a snowblower chute that would dent it in August will crack it in January, and a cracked panel on a Columbus winter wall lets driven snow in behind the siding.

It hides rot instead of fixing it

The most common vinyl job we get called behind is a house that was re-sided over soft sheathing and a failed wrap. New panels over rot buy you a clean look and a worse wall. We strip, inspect and repair the substrate, or we tell you the job is not a siding job.

It is wrong on a brick or stone front

Where a brick or stone facade meets a vinyl wall, thin hollow panels next to a four-inch masonry assembly read cheap from the curb. On those houses we push fiber cement or engineered wood, with trim that has depth.

Common questions

What homeowners ask about vinyl

Can you install vinyl in an Ohio winter?

We can, with care. Cold panels are brittle, so they are handled and cut differently and the work is scheduled for the warmer part of the day. Fiber cement and engineered wood are more forgiving in the cold.

Does vinyl need maintenance?

It does not need paint, which is the main reason people buy it. It does need loose panels re-seated, joints and corners checked after a wind event, and washing where algae grows on a shaded north wall.

Should I do vinyl now and something better later?

Only if the wall is sound and you know you are buying time. Doing the substrate work twice costs more than doing it once, so if a longer-life material is in your plan at all, the siding hub compares the three before you commit.
