Gutter guards

Gutter guards, and when we say you do not need them

We do not sell a guard brand and we are not authorized by one. What follows is how the three common designs behave under Ohio maple seeds, pine needles and a hard summer storm.

Free estimate How matching works

Three designs

Screen, micro-mesh and reverse curve, described generically

Every guard on the market is a variation on one of three ideas. The idea decides what gets through and what still has to be cleaned.

Screen

A perforated or expanded-metal sheet laid over the gutter mouth. Cheap, easy to fit, and it stops whole leaves. It does not stop maple seeds, pine needles or granules, and debris mats on top of it, holding moisture against the gutter lip. Cleaning means lifting the screen off, which most people never do.

Micro-mesh

A fine stainless or polymer mesh on a frame, with openings small enough to stop seeds and needles. This is the design that actually handles an Ohio maple. It still catches a film of silt, pollen and granule dust, and that film has to be brushed or rinsed off or water stops passing through.

Reverse curve

A solid hood with a downward-curving nose. Water follows the curve into a slot while leaves fall off the front. It works in light, even rain. In a downpour, or where a valley concentrates flow, the water overshoots the nose and never enters the gutter. That is not a clog; it is a gutter not collecting.

What lands in your gutter

Central Ohio debris, in the order it arrives

The tree species decide which guard is worth anything.

  • Maple seeds. The samaras that spin down in late spring are the worst local problem: small enough to pass a screen, and they wedge into mesh, then compact into a plug at the outlet.
  • Pine needles. From the pines in older Dublin and Upper Arlington lots. Needles bridge a screen and knit into a thatch that sheds water over the front of the gutter.
  • Oak and maple leaf fall. October and November. Big, obvious, and the only debris a screen genuinely stops.
  • Shingle granules. A new architectural roof sheds granules for its first seasons, and that fine grit is what silts a micro-mesh.
  • Pollen. The spring film turns to paste on any guard surface and is the real reason a micro-mesh still needs attention.

Wind-driven rain off Hoover Reservoir makes this harder on the northeast side of the metro. Water arriving sideways does not follow a reverse-curve nose; it goes over the front of the gutter and down the fascia.

Under a mature maple, no guard removes the need for maintenance. It changes the interval and the kind of work, and a good installer says that plainly instead of implying otherwise.

The honest answer

When we recommend not buying them

These are real cases we talk homeowners out of. We would rather lose the sale than install something that gets blamed on us later.

Few or no trees over the roof

A house on an open lot with nothing overhanging the eave gets a light grit load and a rinse twice a year. A guard there is a maintenance item pretending to remove one.

An undersized gutter

A guard adds no capacity. If the run or outlet is already too small for the roof area, a hood that sheds heavy rain over the nose makes overspill worse. Fix the size first, which is a sizing conversation, not a guard one.

A gutter at the end of its life

Fitting guards to a run with failing seams, sagging pitch or soft fascia fastens new money to old metal. Replace the gutter, then decide about the guard against the new one.

An eave you cannot reach

Every guard needs periodic surface cleaning. If the eave is three stories up or the pitch makes ladder work unreasonable, a design that must be brushed off is a promise nobody keeps.

Where a guard does make sense we fit it to a sound, correctly sized gutter on a house whose tree cover justifies it, and we tell you what the cleaning interval still is. Localized clog and overflow fixes go through the gutter section of our exterior repairs page.
