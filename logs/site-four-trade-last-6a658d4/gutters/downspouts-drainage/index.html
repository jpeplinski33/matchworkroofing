Downspouts and drainage

Downspout extensions and underground tie-ins

A gutter that catches every drop and drops it beside your footing has solved nothing. The downspout and what happens after it are half the job.

Free estimate How matching works

Placement and count

How many downspouts, and where they land

Count follows roof area and run length. A long eave with one downspout mid-run asks all the water to reach a single outlet, and it arrives faster than that outlet can pass it. A second downspout at the far end usually costs less than upsizing the whole gutter.

Placement follows the house. Downspouts go where discharge can get away from the foundation: toward a yard that falls, toward an existing storm line, or toward a side where an extension will not be mowed across.

A downspout emptying into a well at the base of a basement wall is the most common drainage defect we find on Columbus houses, and it is usually an original-construction decision, not anyone's mistake. Two downspouts should not share one patch of ground either. That spot saturates, freezes solid in January, and the next storm runs off the ice straight back to the house.

What happens at the bottom

Splash blocks, extensions and buried tie-ins

Splash blocks

A tray under the elbow. It stops the downspout scouring a hole in the soil and that is all: water moves roughly two feet, the block creeps downhill over the seasons, and a channel washes out beside the foundation.

Surface extensions

Rigid or flexible pipe carrying discharge several feet out onto ground that falls away. Cheap and effective when the yard cooperates. The failure is a flexible extension crushed, disconnected by a mower, or laid flat.

Buried tie-ins

An elbow into solid pipe, trenched below frost influence and run to a daylight outlet, a dry well or an existing storm line. Out of sight, clear of mowing, and the right answer on flat yards or tight lot lines.

Tying into an existing line

Connecting to a storm line, and what to check first

Many older central Ohio houses already have an underground storm line, sometimes tied to a foundation drain, sometimes running to a curb outlet or a rear ditch. Using it is often the cleanest answer, because the pipe and the outlet exist.

Three checks before we connect anything. That the line is open: older clay and cast iron collapses or fills with roots, so we run it and watch the daylight end. That it has fall, with the connection below the elbow, not above it. That it has capacity for the added roof area, because a pipe sized for one downspout serving four backs up through the lowest connection in a hard storm.

If the line fails any of those, the answer is a new run to daylight or a dry well, not a connection that floods the lowest downspout the first July it really rains.

Grade and heave

Grading, foundation water and freeze-thaw at the outlet

Drainage and grading are one system. Water discharged onto ground that slopes back toward the house returns to the foundation however far the pipe runs. Where the grade is flat or falls the wrong way we say so, because a buried tie-in emptying into a bowl is a slower splash block.

Then winter. A discharge point that stays wet through a freeze cycle heaves: soil lifts, pipe shifts, the elbow separates, and in spring the downspout is pouring inches from the wall again. Outlets go on a falling grade and below the frost layer for that reason.

Foundation water is not a gutter problem and we do not claim to solve it from the eave. What we control is that the roof load stops arriving next to your footing.

Knock-on effects

Ice dams start at the eave and finish at the downspout

An ice dam forms when a warm attic melts snow on the upper slope and the meltwater refreezes at the cold eave. The gutter freezes into the dam, water backs up under the shingles, and the downspout stops running because the system is solid ice. When the dam releases it often takes gutter, hangers and a strip of fascia with it.

Drainage feeds the loop from the other end. Saturated soil beside the foundation holds moisture against the wall and into the basement, which raises indoor humidity, which raises attic moisture, which worsens the temperature profile at the eave. Slow, not immediate, but real.

So we treat the two together. The gutter and downspout side is ours. The attic side — ventilation, insulation, air sealing at the top plate — is a roof-side conversation: see roof leak and flashing repair, alongside the eave work in the gutter section of our exterior repairs page. We will tell you which half your problem is in rather than sell you the half we happen to do.
