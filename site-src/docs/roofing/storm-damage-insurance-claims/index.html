What we look at

How a storm inspection works

We mark out a test square on each slope and count hits inside it, which is how hail damage gets measured consistently across a roof. We're looking for hail bruising and blistering on the shingle mat, wind creases where a tab has been lifted and re-sealed, and granule loss that exposes the asphalt underneath.

Test square

A marked sample area on each roof slope, used to count and document hail hits consistently.

Bruising vs. blistering

Hail bruising is impact damage to the mat; blistering is a manufacturing or aging defect. Telling them apart matters for the claim.

Wind creases & granule loss

Creases show where wind lifted a tab past its seal; granule loss exposes the shingle's asphalt layer and shortens its life.

The soft metal on the house gets looked at too, because it corroborates what the shingles are telling us. Dents in gutter aprons, fascia, ridge cap, vent hoods, AC fins and downspouts record the size and the direction of the hail far more legibly than a shingle field does. So do spatter marks on the siding and knock-outs on screens. Direction matters: a storm out of the southwest loads the south and west slopes hardest, and a roof with hits on every slope tells a different story from one with hits on two.

Why it matters

What hail and wind actually do to a shingle

Neither failure is a hole you can see from the driveway, which is why both get missed and both get exaggerated.

A hail strike crushes the asphalt mat underneath the granules. From above it may look like nothing at all, or like a dark spot where the granules have been pressed into the mat. The damage is that the mat is broken at that point, and the granules over it are no longer held properly. Those granules let go over the following seasons, the asphalt underneath is exposed to UV, and the shingle ages at that spot far faster than the rest of the field. That is functional damage: it shortens the roof, and it does so invisibly at first.

Wind does something different. It gets under a tab, lifts it past the point where the seal strip holds, and either takes the tab off or lets it come back down creased. A creased tab has a permanent fold in it, the seal is broken along that fold, and the shingle is finished even though it is still on the roof. The next wind event lifts it further, and the granules at the crease go first.

Both of those are different from normal aging, and telling the difference is the whole job. Granule loss in a valley where water runs, blistering from a manufacturing or ventilation problem, and mechanical damage from a ladder or a branch all look vaguely like storm damage and none of them are. We say which one we are looking at, in writing, and we photograph it.

  • Hail: crushed mat under the granules, granules let go later, asphalt exposed to UV.
  • Wind: tab lifted past its seal, creased on the way back down, seal broken at the fold.
  • Soft metal: dents on gutters, fascia, ridge cap, vent hoods and AC fins corroborate size and direction.
  • Not storm damage: blistering, valley wear, ladder damage, branch strikes, and normal aging.
  • Cosmetic versus functional: we say which one we are looking at rather than leaving you to guess.

The same distinction shows up on the hail damage versus blistering article, which goes through the two side by side.

Working with your adjuster

If the initial estimate misses something we documented, a supplement is simply a follow-up request to add that item to the scope — it's a routine part of the claims process, not a dispute.

The sequence

What happens, in order

So that nothing on this list is a surprise when it arrives.

  1. You report the damage to your carrier

    That is your call and your conversation, and it is a step we do not do for you or on your behalf. Most policies want damage reported promptly, so reporting early costs nothing and protects your position.

  2. We inspect and document

    Test squares on each slope, hits counted and chalked, photographs by slope and by detail, soft metal examined, and the interior and attic checked where they can be reached. You get our findings in writing whether or not a claim is filed.

  3. The carrier sends an adjuster

    We ask to be on the roof at the same time. Two people looking at the same shingle from the same angle is how the facts stay the facts, and it saves a second trip for everybody.

  4. A scope and an estimate

    Written by us, itemized, naming the exact product and color and every flashing being replaced. It is a document you can compare against the carrier's numbers line by line, which is the only sensible way to look at two estimates.

  5. Supplements, where something was missed

    Decking that turns out to be soft, flashing that has to be replaced rather than reused, code-driven ventilation, or a slope that was not walked. A supplement is a routine follow-up request with documentation attached, not a fight and not a red flag.

  6. The work, and the paperwork afterwards

    Scheduled around you, executed with the house protected, and closed out with photographs of the completed roof, the materials installed and the warranty terms that apply. That file goes to you and, where there is a claim, to whoever else needs it.

Two things about this sequence are worth stating twice. We never ask you to sign anything before you have read it, and we never start work against a claim that has not been approved in writing by your carrier.

What homeowners should know

You choose your contractor

Ohio homeowners generally have the right to choose who repairs their home, regardless of who an adjuster or carrier recommends. We're happy to be considered alongside anyone else you're talking to.

What that looks like in practice: you are not obliged to use a contractor a carrier suggests, you are not obliged to use one who knocks on your door the week after a storm, and you are entitled to compare written scopes rather than accept the first number you are given. Read the proposal, compare the materials line by line rather than the totals, and ask questions until the answers make sense to you. If the answer to a question is pressure, that is the answer.

We would rather lose a job to a fair comparison than win one by being the first truck on the street. Door-knocking after a storm is how the worst of this trade behaves, and it is not how we work.

Common questions

What homeowners ask after a storm

I can't see anything from the ground. Is that a good sign?

It is a neutral one. Hail bruising does not show from the ground, and a wind crease on a second slope may not either. Equally, plenty of roofs in a hail-affected neighbourhood have nothing wrong with them, because the hail fell in a band and your street was outside it. The only way to know is to look at the shingles from above.

Should I file a claim before or after you look?

Either order works, and neither one obliges you to do the other. Most policies want damage reported promptly, so reporting it does not hurt. We will inspect whether or not a claim is open, and we will tell you plainly if what we found does not amount to a claim worth filing.

Can you repair part of a storm-damaged roof?

Sometimes, and where the damage is localized to a slope or a detail we will say so rather than quote the whole roof. The honest limit is matching: new shingles against a weathered field will read as new for years, and we will show you the two side by side before you decide. That conversation is on the shingle matching page.

How long do I have?

Damage does not stay static. A creased tab loses granules at the fold, an exposed mat takes UV, and one Ohio winter of freeze-thaw turns a bruise into a leak. If a storm hit and you are still deciding, get the roof looked at and get the findings in writing so the decision is made against evidence rather than against a deadline you cannot see.

Will you meet the adjuster with me?

Yes, and we ask for it rather than wait to be invited. The point is that the same facts are in front of both of you on the same roof on the same day, with the same chalk marks still on the shingles.

What if the claim doesn't cover everything?

Then the scope gets written so you can see exactly what is covered, what is not, and what the uncovered part would cost to do at the same time. We will not quietly drop an item to make a number fit, and we will not tell you an outcome we cannot know.

Storm work is part of the wider Columbus roofing silo, and a single failed flashing or a leak with no storm behind it is a repair call rather than a claim: see roofing repairs in Columbus and the roof leak and flashing repair page. Where a roof is finished and the answer is a new one, that project is described on the roof replacement page, and the color work behind it is on the Matchwork Method page.
