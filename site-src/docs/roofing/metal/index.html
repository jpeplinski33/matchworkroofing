Metal roofing

Standing seam, copper and porch-accent metal roofs

Metal as a whole roof, and metal used where it earns its keep on a shingled house: porches, bays, dormers, valleys and the small roofs nobody thinks about until they leak.

A full metal roof

Standing seam, properly

Panels that clip to the deck and a seam that stands proud of the water. Nothing fastens through the flat of the panel, which is what separates this from a pole-barn roof.

Each panel has formed legs at both edges. Two adjacent legs are joined and seamed above the panel surface, so water runs down the flat and never reaches a penetration. The panel is held by clips screwed to the deck, and the clip is what lets the metal move as it heats and cools. That movement happens on every metal roof, every day; the clip is how the roof survives it.

Because nothing goes through the flat, standing seam tolerates a lower pitch than shingles do. It also sheds snow fast, which is a real consideration on a Columbus house: it comes off in a slab and without warning, so snow retention is part of the design wherever a walkway, deck, driveway or gutter sits below the slope.

Under the panels goes underlayment and, at eaves and valleys, ice-and-water shield. Metal does not remove the need for the layers beneath it, and a metal roof laid on bare boards leaks like any other.

  • Concealed fasteners — nothing penetrates the flat of the panel.
  • Clips rather than screws, so the metal can expand and contract.
  • Seams folded or mechanically locked above the drainage plane.
  • Works at pitches shingles cannot, which suits porches and additions.
  • Snow retention specified where anything sits below the slope.
  • Underlayment and ice-and-water shield underneath, same as any roof.

Near-flat sections are a different assembly again. That work is on the flat and low-slope roofing page, in EPDM, TPO, PVC and modified bitumen.

Metal as a detail

Porch, bay and dormer accents

Most of the metal we install here is not a whole roof. It is the small roof that keeps failing on a house whose field shingles are fine.

Front porch roofs

A porch roof sits at a shallow pitch and catches runoff from the main roof above it. Metal handles the pitch and the volume; shingles at that slope do not.

Bay windows and turrets

A bay roof is a set of short, tight facets that shingles cover badly. Metal can be formed to the shape and seamed at the hips, which is both watertight and the detail the house was drawn to have.

Dormers

Dormer cheeks and sidewalls are where water concentrates. A metal dormer roof with step flashing into the shingle field ends a recurring leak instead of re-caulking it.

Valleys

An open metal valley moves more water than a woven shingle valley and reads as a deliberate line up the roof face. Copper valley metal is the traditional choice and outlasts the shingles either side.

An accent roof that fights the main roof is a mistake you look at for decades. Panel color and finish get held against the shingle, the trim and the gutters on site, in daylight, before anything is ordered — the same method described on the Matchwork Method page.

Copper

Copper roofing, and copper where it counts

Copper is both a roofing material and a detail material, and on most houses we install it as the second. A full copper roof is rare in this metro and copper is a premium material, well above standing seam and architectural shingles in cost. A copper bay roof, porch, valley, chimney cap or cricket, or copper gutters and downspouts are the places it usually belongs.

What you are buying is longevity and a finish that changes. New copper is bright and reads warm against brick, then darkens, then moves toward a green-brown patina — slower on a shaded north slope than on a south one. That cannot be rushed honestly. If you want the roof to look consistent on the day it is finished, copper is the wrong choice. If you want a detail that outlasts every shingle roof it sits beside, it is the right one.

Copper also has to be detailed against dissimilar metals, because its runoff is hard on some other metal products. Transitions, fasteners and the gutter below get specified together. Copper gutters and downspouts are specified on the same proposal, described on the gutters hub.

The honest part

Noise, movement, denting and cost

Four things every metal roof does. Better to hear them now than in the first October rain.

It is louder, but not how you think

Metal over an open frame with no decking and no attic below is loud. Metal over solid decking with an insulated attic under it is a moderate drumming in heavy rain and close to silent in light rain. The difference is the assembly, not the metal. On a porch with an open ceiling you will hear it, and we will say so before you choose it.

It expands and contracts

Metal moves with temperature, a lot, every day. Screws through the flat of a panel fight that movement and eventually oval the hole, which is how a good material becomes a leaking roof. Floating or sliding clips are non-negotiable. Some roofs also tick after sunrise; that is normal and it settles.

It can dent

Large hail dents metal, and a dent in a flat panel is far more visible than bruising in a shingle. Thicker gauge and a striated or textured finish hide it better than a smooth one. Branches and carelessly leaned ladders dent it too. Metal is a different failure mode from shingles, and a visible one.

On cost, stated as a band and never as a number: standing seam sits above architectural shingles, well above on a complicated roofline with a lot of trim and flashing, and copper sits above standing seam. We measure yours, price the actual assembly in writing, and show you where the money goes — panels, trim, clips, or the substructure built before any of it goes on.

How it gets installed

The transitions are the job

A metal roof does not fail in the middle of a panel. It fails where it meets something else: the shingle field above a porch, a dormer sidewall, a chimney, a skylight curb, a gutter, or the rake edge where the panel ends.

So that is where the work goes. Headwall flashing above the porch roof, step flashing at every sidewall, formed trim at rakes and eaves, closure strips where a panel ends open, and counterflashing cut into masonry where metal meets brick. Each detail is named on the proposal with its material, rather than left to whoever is on the roof that morning.

Where metal work touches a chimney or a brick wall, that is a two-trade detail. The masonry side — counterflashing, cricket, cap and mortar — is on the chimney page, and both sides sit on one Matchwork proposal, sequenced so the metal and the masonry are not waiting on each other.

  • Deck checked and re-nailed before anything is clipped down.
  • Underlayment, plus ice-and-water shield at eaves, valleys and penetrations.
  • Clips set on layout lines, panels squared to the ridge before they are seamed.
  • Seams folded in the same direction up the whole slope.
  • Trim, closures and transitions specified in writing before the order goes in.

Common questions

What homeowners ask about metal

Can metal go over my existing shingles?

We do not recommend it and will not quote it as the good option. A roof-over hides the deck, keeps the old flashing and gives the clips nothing sound to hold. We tear off so we can see what we are fastening to.

Does metal attract lightning?

No. A metal roof does not make a strike more likely. It is non-combustible, which is a different and more useful property in a storm.

Will an accent roof match the house?

That is the point of bringing samples to the house. Panel color and finish get held against the shingle field, trim, gutters and brick in your daylight, twice, before it is ordered.

A leaking porch roof, a failed bay or a dormer that was never right is a repair, not a re-roof. Our roofing repairs in Columbus cover flashing, transitions and the small roofs.
