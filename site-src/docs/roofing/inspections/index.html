Roof inspection

A roof inspection that tells you what you have

Not a sales call with a ladder. A look at the whole roof from the ground, from the deck and from the attic, and a written account of what we found.

What is actually looked at

Three vantage points, in order

Ground, roof and attic. Each shows failures the other two cannot, and skipping one is how a roof gets misdiagnosed.

From the ground

Sight lines along each slope for sagging, uneven coursing and dipping valleys. Ridge straightness, chimney lean, gutter slope and where the downspouts discharge. Fascia and soffit staining, and moss patterns, which tell you which slopes stay wet.

On the roof

The field: granule loss, curling, wind creases, hail bruising, exposed fasteners, course alignment. Then every flashing — chimney step and counterflashing, sidewalls, dormers, valleys, pipe boots, vent hoods, skylight curbs, drip edge, kickouts — and anything sealed over where metal should have been cut in.

In the attic

Where access allows: the underside of the decking for staining, mould and rusted nail shanks; insulation depth and whether it blocks intake at the eaves; airflow from soffit to ridge and whether it is balanced; and daylight through the deck, which finds openings nobody can see from above.

Some roofs cannot be safely walked and some attics cannot be entered. When that happens we name it in the findings rather than quietly leaving it out. That limitation carries into the written report too — see the dated roof inspection report page.

What you get

Written findings, photographs, and three buckets

You should be able to read the result without us standing next to you explaining it.

At the end you get a written account organised by slope, with photographs of what we found and a plain-English statement of the roof's condition as observed on the day. Every item goes into one of three buckets, and the bucket is the useful part.

  • Fix now. An active failure, or a detail that will fail before the next winter, named specifically.
  • Watch. Something beginning but not yet a problem: where it is, what it looks like now, what change would mean it has moved.
  • Nothing wrong. Stated as clearly as the other two. A roof with life left in it gets a report that says so.

If you then want work done, the scope and its price are a separate document written from the findings. We do not hand you a number on a ladder and we do not treat an inspection as the opening of a sales conversation.

Two different jobs

How this differs from a home inspection

A general home inspector and a roofing contractor are doing different work on the same house, and neither replaces the other.

A home inspector walks the whole property: structure, electrical, plumbing, heating and cooling, interior and site. The roof is one line item among many, viewed largely from the ground and the edge of the eaves, and reported as a general condition remark. That is the job, and it is a valuable one.

A roofing inspection goes onto the roof and into the attic, looks at every flashing and penetration individually, and reports the roof as an assembly: field, flashings, deck, ventilation, and how they perform together.

Where a home inspection has already named the roof — curling tabs, a ceiling stain, a remark that it is at or near the end of its serviceable life — a roofing inspection turns that remark into something a buyer, a seller or an agent can act on.

We do not override the home inspector and we do not claim to. We translate the finding into roofing terms and separate what genuinely has to be done before a closing from what can be monitored. That triage is the whole of our Close-Ready program for realtors, and it is available to a homeowner with no transaction attached.

If you are buying or selling, the document you may be asked for is a dated report — a different deliverable with different wording and different limits, described on the roof inspection report for a home sale page.

When to book one

Four situations where an inspection pays for itself

After a storm

Hail bruising and wind creases are hard to see from the ground and easy to overstate from a ladder. An inspection tells you whether anything is there before you decide what to do. The claims side is on the storm damage page.

Before you list, or right after you buy

Before listing, finding a flashing failure yourself is the difference between a small repair in October and a renegotiation in November. After buying, you get a dated baseline of photographs and written condition, so anything that changes later is measured against something real.

When a quote feels wrong

If you have been told you need a full replacement and something does not sit right, an independent look at the same roof is a reasonable thing to want. We will tell you if the other quote was right.

Before something is fastened to it

Solar, a dormer, an addition, an antenna mount or a heavy new gutter run. Anything that puts fasteners into a roof deserves a look at the roof first, and older slate and cedar fields fail in specific, visible ways worth catching early.

The limits

What an inspection is not

  • Not a warranty on the roof. Nothing in it promises the roof will not leak later.
  • Not an estimate of remaining life in years. We describe what we observe and put no clock on it.
  • Not an appraisal or a valuation of the property.
  • Blind to concealed conditions: water in a wall cavity, decking under a second layer, framing behind finished surfaces.
  • A statement about the day it was performed. Weather after that date is not covered by it.
  • Not a promise that a lender, a buyer or a property insurer will accept any conclusion in it.

Those limits are stated because they are the part most often left out, and because a homeowner who understands them can use the findings properly.

What an inspection is, is an honest account of your roof's condition on a specific day, from someone who works on roofs for a living, in writing, with photographs, and with no sales pitch attached.

If what you have is one active leak or one failed flashing, that is a repair call rather than an inspection call. Common questions

What homeowners ask about inspections

How long does it take?

Longer than a drive-by, shorter than you would expect for the amount of looking involved. Roof size, pitch, penetrations and attic access all change it, and the findings are written up after we are off the roof.

Will you walk on my roof?

Where it is safe to and the material allows it. On slate and cedar we are far more careful, because a boot cracks a slate and curls a shake. When we cannot walk it, we say so and name what we could not see.

Do you inspect against another contractor's quote?

Yes. Where another contractor has quoted a replacement, we will look at the same roof and tell you whether we agree — including when we do and the other quote was right.
