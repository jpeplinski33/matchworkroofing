Skylights and flashing

Skylights, curb flashing and the leaks they cause

A skylight is a hole in a roof with a window in it. Most of what goes wrong is not the window — it is the curb, the flashing around it, or water that was never a leak at all.

Two assemblies

Curb-mounted versus deck-mounted

The difference is whether the skylight sits on a raised frame or directly on the roof deck, and it decides how the flashing works.

A curb-mounted skylight sits on a timber or metal frame built up off the deck and sheathed in plywood. The roofing material is flashed up the sides of the curb, and the unit is then set on top and sealed to it with a gasket or compression flange. The curb lifts the glazing clear of the water running down the roof, which is the whole point, and it can be repaired or rebuilt without opening the roof again.

A deck-mounted skylight has no curb. It carries an integral flange that lies flat against the deck and is flashed into the shingle courses with a factory kit. It sits lower and reads cleaner from the ground, and it depends entirely on that kit being installed in the right sequence with the right lap. It is common on newer stock and fails almost always at the kit: a reused kit, a kit installed out of order, or a kit that was never the one for that shingle.

Telling them apart from the ground is easy. A vertical face below the glazing means a curb; glass sitting almost flush with the field means deck-mounted.

  • Curb-mounted: raised frame, roofing flashed up its sides, unit sealed to the top.
  • Deck-mounted: integral flange on the deck, flashed in with a factory kit.
  • The curb lifts the glazing clear of runoff — the assembly's main advantage.
  • A curb can be rebuilt without opening the whole roof again.
  • A deck-mounted kit is a one-time part. It does not get reused.
  • Low pitches want a curb. Very low pitches want something that is not a skylight.

A skylight on a near-flat porch or addition roof is a different problem, described on the flat and low-slope roofing page.

Where the water gets in

Curb flashing, done in order

Four sides, four different details. Sealant is not one of them.

Downhill and sides

Step flashing, one piece per shingle course, lapped so water is handed from one piece of metal to the next on its way down and woven into the shingles rather than laid over them. A single continuous piece is a shortcut and it fails at the shingle lap.

The uphill side

This is the side that leaks. It needs a headwall flashing and, on anything but a shallow slope, a saddle or cricket built above the skylight to split the runoff around it instead of letting a full slope of water hit the top of the curb.

Shield underneath

A penetration in the deck is where an ice dam backs water up, and a skylight is a large one. Ice-and-water shield under the flashing at all four sides is not an upgrade; it is how the opening is protected while the metal does its job.

The unit-to-curb seal, and the shaft

Gaskets and compression flanges dry out and shrink on a south-facing Ohio roof with full UV on them, and this seal fails far more often than the flashing does. Below it, the light shaft through the attic is usually uninsulated and unvented, collects condensation, and gets blamed for a leak it did not cause.

A drip or a stain at a skylight is diagnosed the same way as any other roof leak, and that method is on the roof leak and flashing repair page: inside from the attic, uphill on the roof, then reproduced with controlled water before anything is quoted.

The one that is not a leak

Condensation versus leakage

Plenty of what reaches us as a leaking skylight is condensation, and telling the two apart changes the repair completely.

Condensation forms on the cold side of the glazing in winter, runs down the inside face, and collects at the bottom of the unit or on the shaft ceiling. It is worse in a bathroom or kitchen, worse on cold clear nights, worse where the shaft is uninsulated, and it happens with no rain at all. Its staining is soft-edged and it recurs in the same season. The fix is the shaft, the interior humidity and sometimes the glazing unit — not flashing.

Leakage tracks to weather. It follows rain, often rain from one direction, or a thaw after a snowfall. It appears at the top or one side of the ceiling opening rather than evenly around it, it is worse under wind-driven rain, and it leaves a hard-edged stain with a tide line. That is flashing, a failed gasket or a cracked unit.

  • No rain, cold night, bathroom or kitchen below: suspect condensation.
  • Rain from one direction, stain at the top or one side: suspect flashing.
  • After a thaw, at the uphill side: suspect an ice dam behind the unit.
  • Drip from the frame on a hot day: suspect a failed glazing gasket.
  • Stain spread evenly around the opening: suspect the shaft, not the roof.

Repair or replace

When the unit comes out

A gasket, a section of step flashing or a saddle can be repaired in place, and where the unit is sound that is the right scope. Where the unit has failed — a fogged or cracked pane, a frame that has lost its seal, a curb rotting underneath — replacement is cheaper than the second call.

When we replace, we do not reuse the old flashing kit and we do not set a new unit on an old curb without looking at it. The curb gets opened and its sheathing checked, anything soft replaced, shield set at all four sides, new step flashing woven into the existing courses, a new saddle on the uphill face, and a new unit set and sealed on top.

  • New unit, new flashing kit, every time. Kits are not reusable.
  • Curb opened and sheathing checked before anything is set back on it.
  • Ice-and-water shield at all four sides of the opening.
  • Step flashing woven into the courses, one piece per shingle lap.
  • Saddle or cricket on the uphill face wherever the pitch wants one.
  • Surrounding shingles matched on site, the repair broken at a course line.

Matching matters more here than on almost any other detail, because a skylight sits in the middle of a visible slope. That process is on the shingle matching page. Where skylights are one part of a roof that is finished, the honest scope is the whole roof: see the roof replacement page.

Straight terms

What we warrant

What we warrant

Matchwork warrants the repairs we performed, on the terms in your proposal. A repair is not a warranty on the rest of the roof, the siding, the gutters or the masonry we did not touch, and we will never present it as one.

On a skylight that means: if we rebuilt the curb, set new flashing and sealed a new unit, and that skylight leaks, it is ours to come back to. A different skylight dripping next winter is a separate problem on a roof we did not replace, and we treat it as one.

It also means we will not sell you a gasket against a rotted curb, and we will not tell you a flashing repair fixes condensation. Both happen, and both leave the homeowner paying twice.

Common questions

What homeowners ask about skylights

Can I just seal around it?

Sealant over a failed flashing detail is temporary and usually makes the real repair harder, because the next person has to clean it all off to see underneath. We use sealant where it belongs — at the unit-to-curb joint — and nowhere it does not.

How long does a skylight last?

The glazing unit and the flashing have different lives, and on a south-facing Ohio slope the gasket usually goes first. We do not put a number of years on it; we look at the unit, the curb and the metal and tell you which of the three is finished.

Should I remove it and roof over the hole?

Sometimes that is the honest answer, particularly on a low pitch or where the shaft is uninsulated and condensing. Closing the opening means framing the deck back in, matching the shingle field and dealing with the ceiling below, and we scope all three.
