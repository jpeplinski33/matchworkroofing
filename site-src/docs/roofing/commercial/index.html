Commercial roofing

Multifamily, HOA, church and small commercial roofs

Occupied buildings, several decision-makers, a budget cycle, and a roof that has to be phased around the people underneath it. Different work from a single-family re-roof, and different paperwork.

Free estimate How matching works

Who this page is for

Boards, committees, managers and trustees

The person signing is rarely the person who found the leak, and the document has to work for both of them.

Most of the non-residential roofing we do in this metro is not large commercial work. It is a townhome block with a shared valley that leaks into two units. It is an association's common element — a clubhouse, a gatehouse, a maintenance building. It is a congregational building with a steep nave roof, a low-slope addition behind it, and a facilities committee that meets monthly. It is a small commercial strip, a light industrial unit, or a mixed-use building with a flat roof over the back and a pitched one over the front.

What all of those have in common is that the decision is made by a group, the money comes from a budget that was set earlier in the year, and the building is occupied while the work happens. So the deliverable is not only a roof. It is a written scope a board can vote on, a phasing plan a property manager can post, and closeout documentation a reserve study or a future manager can read.

We describe the building types we work on rather than naming buildings, associations or congregations. If you want references for a specific kind of structure, ask and we will answer that question directly and privately.

Building types

What we take on

Described by structure, because the structure decides the assembly and the access plan.

Multifamily and townhome blocks

Repeating rooflines with shared valleys, party-wall separations, and units occupied on both sides of the work. Phasing unit by unit or block by block, with notice posted ahead of each section and a dry-in plan for anything opened up.

Association common elements

Clubhouses, gatehouses, pool structures, maintenance buildings and the entrance features an association is responsible for. Small footprints, visible from the street, and subject to the association's own standards and approved materials.

Congregational buildings

Steep nave roofs, towers, low-slope additions, and often a mixture of materials that were added at different times. Work scheduled around services and events, with a facilities committee as the point of contact rather than a single owner.

Small commercial and light industrial

Low-slope membrane roofs over retail strips, offices, workshops and storage. Drainage, rooftop units, penetrations and traffic wear are the usual subjects, and the membrane choice follows what is under the roof and what goes onto it.

Mixed-use and storefront buildings

A pitched roof over the front, a flat one over the back, parapets, and a cornice or façade that has to be protected while the roof above it is worked on. Two assemblies and one building, scoped as one project.

Envelope work around the roof

Gutters and downspouts on a large roof, siding and trim at the transition, masonry at parapets and chimneys. One crew for the whole envelope means one scope, one schedule and one answerable person rather than three handoffs.

The flat and low-slope assemblies themselves — EPDM, TPO, PVC and modified bitumen, and what each one is good for — are described on the flat and low-slope roofing page. Gutters and drainage at scale are on the gutters hub, and masonry at parapets and chimneys is on the masonry hub.

What is different

Access, phasing and the paperwork

Five things a residential re-roof never has to deal with, and all five are decided before the first material is ordered.

  • Occupied buildings. People live, work or gather underneath. Work hours, noise, dust, and the perimeter around the building get planned rather than improvised, and the plan gets posted where the occupants will see it.
  • Phasing. A block is not done all at once. Sections are sequenced so nothing is opened up overnight, so dry-in is achievable at the end of every work day, and so the units that are affected are known in advance.
  • Access and staging. Where the dumpster goes, where the pallets are set, which side of the building the lift can reach, what has to be protected at ground level, and who has to be told before the crew arrives.
  • Decision-making. A board or committee votes on a document, not on a conversation. The scope is written so a member who has never been on the roof can follow what is being approved and what is being excluded.
  • Budget and reserve cycles. Work often has to be scoped to a number that was set months ago, or split across two cycles. Where that is the case we will write the scope in separable sections so a board can choose the order rather than being handed one take-it-or-leave-it figure.
  • Closeout documentation. What was done, where, with what material, on what date, photographed. It goes to the manager and the board, and it is what the next person reads when the question comes up again.

Manufacturer warranty terms on a non-residential structure differ from the terms on a single-family detached house, and we do not quote them from memory. Whatever coverage applies to your building is confirmed and written into the proposal before a vote is taken.

How a project runs

From a site walk to a board package

  1. A site walk with whoever is responsible

    A manager, a facilities chair, a trustee or a board member walks it with us. Roofs, transitions, drainage, gutters, the masonry and siding that meet the roof, and the interior ceilings below where they can be reached. We ask what has been done before and what happened afterwards.

  2. A written scope, in separable sections

    What is failing, what is proposed, what material and what attachment method, what is deliberately excluded, and what the phasing looks like. Where a budget has to be split, the scope is written so sections can be approved in an order the board chooses.

  3. A phasing and access plan

    Sections in sequence, work hours, staging and dumpster locations, perimeter protection, dry-in at the end of each day, and the notice text that goes to occupants. The manager gets something they can post rather than something they have to translate.

  4. Execution with one answerable person

    One point of contact for the building, from first day to final walk. Changes, additions and anything found once the roof is opened go through that person in writing, and the board is not left to reconstruct what happened from an invoice.

  5. Closeout and documentation

    A final walk with the responsible party, photographs of the completed work by section, the materials installed and their specification, the warranty terms that apply to that building type, and the paperwork filed with the association or the property record.

Across a whole block

Matching identical units

A townhome block is the hardest matching problem we work on, because every unit was built from the same palette at the same time and every roof has weathered slightly differently since.

Re-roofing one unit in a block means the new roof sits beside roofs that have faded, and the association usually wants to know whether that difference will show from the street and whether it will close up. Sometimes it will. Sometimes the honest answer is that it will not, and the only way to make it consistent is to schedule the neighbouring sections together.

The other half of this is governance. Associations keep approved material lists and colour standards, and some communities have design review that has to be satisfied before an order goes in. We check that before anything is ordered, not after the pallets are on the site, and we submit what the association asks for. The full method is on the Matchwork Method page.

  • Existing roof sampled and compared against current production, not against a name.
  • Association approved lists and colour standards checked before the order.
  • Design review submissions prepared where the community requires them.
  • Neighbouring sections scheduled together where consistency matters.
  • Discontinued products handled honestly: closest available blend, and where the break line falls.

Where a block has been patched by different contractors over the years, the first job is often an inspection that establishes what is actually up there. That is described on the roof inspection page.

Common questions

What boards and managers ask

Can you write a scope our board can vote on?

That is the deliverable. Plain language, sectioned so it can be split across budget cycles, with what is excluded stated as clearly as what is included, and photographs of the conditions the proposal is responding to.

Do you work around our schedule?

Yes, and that is the point of the phasing plan. Services, events, occupancy and unit access all get written into the sequence before work starts. We do not put a date on a proposal until the building's calendar has been looked at.

What happens if something is found underneath?

Decking, framing and concealed conditions get photographed, written up and priced as a change through the single point of contact before work continues on that section. Nothing is covered up and nothing is added verbally.

Small repairs on a commercial or association building are handled the same way as the larger work, with the same documentation. Our roofing repairs in Columbus cover leaks, flashing and storm damage, and a full tear-off and rebuild is described on the roof replacement page.
