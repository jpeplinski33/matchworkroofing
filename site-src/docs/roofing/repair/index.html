Leaks, flashing and storm repairs

Leaks, flashing and storm repairs, found properly

A stain on a ceiling is the end of the story, not the beginning of it. Water runs down rafters, along decking and behind flashing before it shows up inside, so the repair goes where the water got in.

Diagnosis

How we actually find a leak

Three vantage points, in order: inside from the deck, then on top of the roof, working uphill. Skipping one is how a leak gets patched twice and fixed never.

Inside first. In the attic, where it can be reached, we look at the underside of the decking for staining, darkened wood, mold on sheathing, wet insulation and rust on nail shanks. That tells us how far the water traveled before it dropped, which is usually several feet. A stain on a bedroom ceiling under a bathroom vent tells us almost nothing about where the roof is open.

Then the roof, uphill from the stain. Along the way every penetration and every change of plane gets checked — chimneys, sidewalls, dormers, valleys, pipe boots, vents, skylights, the headwall above a porch — and the shingle field for lifted tabs, creases, granule loss, popped fasteners and flashing that was never installed. Where a leak is intermittent and nothing obvious shows, we isolate it: controlled water applied low on the slope and worked upward until it reproduces. Slow, and the only way to be sure the repair we quote is the one you need.

  1. Inside, from the attic

    Deck staining, wet insulation, mold on sheathing, rusted nail shanks — the evidence of how far the water moved.

  2. Uphill on the roof

    Every penetration and change of plane between the ridge and the interior stain, plus the field either side.

  3. Reproduce it, then write it down

    Controlled water where nothing obvious shows, then photographs from all three vantage points and a scope you see before we touch anything.

Where roofs actually leak

Flashing failures, one by one

The middle of a shingle field almost never leaks. Everything below does, and each one fails in its own way.

Chimney flashing

Step flashing up the sides, a cricket on the uphill face, and counterflashing cut into the mortar joint rather than bent over the brick and sealed. Sealed-over counterflashing is the repair we get called back to most often. Where the brick or mortar is the problem, that half is on the chimney page and we do both sides.

Step flashing at sidewalls

Where a roof meets a wall — a dormer cheek, a sidewall, a porch headwall — each course needs its own piece of step flashing lapped with the shingle below. One continuous piece, or sealant in place of metal, lets water behind the siding into the wall cavity.

Valleys

A valley carries two slopes of water down one line. Open metal fails at the seam or where shingles were cut into it; woven and closed-cut valleys fail where the underlayment was never upgraded beneath. We rebuild it with shield under it and the right surface over it.

Pipe boots and vent flashings

The rubber gasket on a plumbing vent boot dries out and cracks with UV, usually well before the shingles around it are done. A small, cheap part, and one of the commonest sources of a hallway or bathroom ceiling stain. We replace the boot, not just the gasket.

Kickout flashing and headwalls

Where a slope ends at a sidewall and a gutter begins, a kickout diverts runoff into the gutter instead of down behind the siding. Missing kickouts rot wall sheathing and never show as a roof leak at all, which is why they go unnoticed for years.

Ice dam at the eaves

Snow melts on a warm upper slope, refreezes at the cold eave, and the dam backs water under the shingles. The repair is ice-and-water shield at the eave plus a ventilation and insulation correction in the attic. Fixing only the shingle leaves the mechanism in place.

Skylights have their own failures — curb-mounted versus deck-mounted, curb flashing, and condensation mistaken for leakage. Those are on the skylight replacement and flashing page.

After weather

Wind and hail repairs

Straight-line wind lifts tabs past their seal strip and leaves a crease where the shingle folded and came back down. A creased tab is finished: it will not re-seal properly and it sheds granules at the fold. Missing tabs, lifted tabs that can still be re-sealed and exposed nail heads get handled individually rather than by replacing a whole slope.

Hail leaves bruising — a dark spot where the mat under the granules has been crushed — rather than a hole. It is easy to miss from the ground and easy to overstate from a ladder, which is why we count it inside a marked test square on each slope and photograph what we find.

Active water gets tarped and dried first, with the temporary protection written down separately from the permanent repair. If a claim is going to be filed, the documentation side is its own page: storm damage and insurance claims.

Straight terms

What a repair covers

What we warrant

Matchwork warrants the repairs we performed, on the terms in your proposal. A repair is not a warranty on the rest of the roof, the siding, the gutters or the masonry we did not touch, and we will never present it as one.

That is the whole of it, and it is stated this way on every repair page on this site, because it is the sentence most often left out of a roofing proposal.

In practice: if we replace a pipe boot and that boot leaks, it is ours. If a different boot leaks on the other side of the roof next spring, that is a new problem on an old roof and we treat it as one — diagnosed the same way, quoted the same way, not presented as a failure of the first repair.

It also means we will not sell a repair against a roof that is finished. Where the field is at the end of its life, a flashing repair is a holding action, and we say so in writing before you spend anything.

The honest call

When the answer is a replacement

  • Failures across more than one detail and more than one slope.
  • Granule loss across the field, not at one impact point.
  • Deck softening found from the attic side in several places.
  • A discontinued shingle product, so a repair cannot be matched.
  • The same leak returned after a competent repair, which points at the assembly.

Where two or three of those are true, a repair is spending money to delay a decision. We tell you, we write down what we saw, and we scope the replacement instead.

A replacement is a different project: a full tear-off to the deck, the deck inspected and re-nailed, then the system rebuilt in order — leak barrier, underlayment, starter course, field shingles, ridge and balanced ventilation. Layer by layer, that is on the full roof replacement page. If what you have is one detail on a roof with life left in it, none of that applies.

Repairs that cross into gutters, siding or masonry are scoped together rather than in three visits. Our exterior repairs in Columbus page covers all four trades, and the wider silo is on the Columbus roofing hub.

Common questions

What homeowners ask about a leaking roof

The stain is in one room. Is the leak right above it?

Usually not. Water runs along the underside of decking and down rafters before it drops, and it can travel several feet. We work uphill from the stain on the roof, not straight up from it.

It only leaks in wind-driven rain. Does that matter?

Yes, and it narrows things down. A leak that only appears with wind points at a step flashing, a headwall, a valley edge or a vent hood that vertical rain never loads. Tell us which direction the storm came from.

Will a repair show against the rest of my roof?

On a weathered field, sometimes. Replacement shingles are matched on site rather than picked from a name, and the repair is broken at a ridge, valley or plane change. When the difference will be visible, we show you the two side by side.
