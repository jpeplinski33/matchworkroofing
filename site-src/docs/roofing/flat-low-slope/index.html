Flat and low-slope

EPDM, TPO, PVC and modified bitumen

A roof that does not shed water fast enough needs a different assembly. Four membranes do that work in central Ohio, and they are not interchangeable.

The physics

Why a shingle roof does not work here

Shingles are a drainage roof. A membrane is a waterproofing roof. The difference is how long water is allowed to sit.

An overlapping shingle field assumes water runs off promptly and never travels uphill. Below a certain pitch it does neither: water slows down, gets driven back up under a course by wind, and sits against laps that were only ever meant to shed. That is why a shingle roof on a near-flat porch leaks, and why it keeps leaking after it has been re-caulked twice.

A membrane assembly assumes the opposite. It is a continuous sheet, seamed into a single plane, with the water directed to a drain, a scupper or a gutter by a designed fall rather than by gravity alone. Where the fall is not enough, tapered insulation is laid under the membrane to create it. Ponding water is the enemy of every flat roof, and a properly built one is designed so that water has somewhere to go.

Nothing here is exotic. It is simply a different trade discipline: fewer components, larger sheets, seams that matter more than anything else on the roof, and edges and penetrations that have to be terminated in metal rather than tucked under a course.

  • Shingles shed water by overlap. A membrane holds water out by continuity.
  • Below a certain pitch, wind drives water back up under a shingle lap.
  • A designed fall, or tapered insulation, moves water to a drain or scupper.
  • Seams are the weak point on every membrane roof, so they are where the work goes.
  • Edges terminate in metal edge strip and termination bar, not in sealant.
  • Ponding water shortens any membrane. Designing it out is cheaper than fixing it later.

A pitched roof with a metal panel on it is a different assembly again, and it is described on the metal roofing page.

Four materials

EPDM, TPO, PVC and modified bitumen

Each one suits a particular roof, a particular budget and a particular kind of use. Here is the honest version of each.

EPDM

A synthetic rubber sheet, usually black, laid in large pieces with few seams. It is the long-established choice on residential low-slope work: forgiving, repairable, and available in wide sheets that mean a porch roof can be covered with one piece. Black EPDM absorbs heat and gets hot, which is a consideration under a living space. Seams are adhered or taped rather than welded, so seam quality depends entirely on the installer's preparation and cleanliness.

TPO

A single-ply thermoplastic sheet, usually white, with heat-welded seams. The weld is the advantage: a properly welded seam is as strong as the sheet either side of it, and it can be tested. White reflects rather than absorbs, which matters over a conditioned space in an Ohio July. It is less tolerant of a poorly prepared substrate than EPDM, and cheap TPO has a reputation for failing early, so the manufacturer and the thickness get specified rather than assumed.

PVC

A single-ply thermoplastic, welded like TPO, and the more chemically resistant of the two. It is the right answer where the roof sits under or beside something that puts oils, grease or chemical runoff onto it — a restaurant exhaust, a mechanical unit, a vehicle-related building. It carries a premium over TPO and EPDM and on a straightforward residential porch it is usually more roof than the job needs.

Modified bitumen

Asphalt-based sheets, reinforced with a mat, laid in multiple plies and joined by torch, hot adhesive or cold adhesive, usually finished with a granule surface. It is tough under foot traffic and it is what a lot of older Columbus flat roofs already have. Torch-applied work on a timber deck carries obvious fire risk and is handled accordingly, with a fire watch and no exceptions. On a small residential porch it is often heavier and more labor than the job wants.

Which one goes on your roof depends on the deck, the pitch, what sits below it, what will walk on it, and what is already there. We specify it in writing on the proposal, with the manufacturer, the thickness and the attachment method named, rather than leaving it as a category.

Where we see it

Porches, additions, mid-century stock and small commercial

Most of the flat and low-slope work on a house in this metro is not the main roof. It is the roof nobody thinks about until it stains a ceiling.

  • Front and rear porch roofs, usually shallow, usually catching runoff from the main roof above, and usually the first thing to fail on an otherwise sound house.
  • Additions, sunrooms and garage roofs, where the new work meets the old at a headwall and the transition is where the water gets in.
  • Mid-century ranch and split-level stock in parts of Columbus, built with deliberately low slopes, flat sections over entries, and carports.
  • Dormer and bay roofs too shallow for shingles, and the small flat sections behind parapets.
  • Small commercial and light industrial buildings, association buildings and outbuildings, where the roof is large, access matters and the work has to be phased.

The larger end of that list — multifamily, association, congregational and small commercial buildings — has its own page, because the conversation there is about access, phasing and paperwork rather than material: multifamily, HOA, church and small commercial roofs.

How it gets built

Substrate, fall, seams, terminations

A membrane is only as good as what is under it and how its edges are held.

Deck and substrate

The deck is checked for deflection, rot and fastener pull-out before anything goes on it. A membrane will telegraph every dip and every screw head underneath it, and a soft deck will fail the seam above it. Cover board or a new substrate goes in where the existing one is not fit.

Insulation and fall

Rigid insulation, tapered where the roof needs a fall created, laid to a plan so water moves to the drain or scupper instead of sitting. Insulation also matters for what is underneath: a porch roof over a conditioned room and a carport roof over open air are different assemblies.

Attachment

Fully adhered, mechanically attached, or ballasted. Adhered is the usual residential choice on a small roof: no fasteners through the membrane, and a sheet that lies flat. Mechanically attached suits larger decks. Ballast is rare on a house and needs a structure designed to carry it.

Seams

Adhered and rolled on EPDM, heat-welded on TPO and PVC, torched or mopped on modified bitumen. Seams are made on clean, dry, warm material and they are checked afterwards — a weld gets tested, an adhered seam gets probed. This is the single step most often rushed.

Terminations and edges

Metal edge strip at perimeters, termination bar at walls with the membrane turned up and sealed into it, and counterflashing cut into masonry rather than bent over the face of a brick. Where a flat roof meets a shingle roof above, the headwall transition is designed and drawn before it is built.

Drainage and protection

Drains, scuppers and gutters sized and kept clear, with a visible overflow path so a blocked drain does not become a flooded roof. Walk pads at hatches and mechanical units, so the membrane is not worn through by the same footpath every time somebody goes up there.

What goes wrong

Five failures, in the order we find them

  1. A blocked drain or scupper

    Leaves and granules dam the outlet, water backs up, and the roof ponds. Ponding water finds every small defect at once and adds weight the deck was not designed for.

  2. An open seam

    A seam that was made on a damp or dirty sheet, or a weld that was never tested. Water enters at the seam and travels sideways under the membrane, so the stain inside is rarely underneath the failure.

  3. A failed wall termination

    Termination bar that has pulled, sealant that has cracked, or counterflashing that was bent over brick rather than cut into a joint. This is the headwall above a porch, and it is the most common leak on a residential flat section.

  4. Punctures and traffic wear

    Ladders, boots, dropped tools, and the same path walked to a hatch for a decade. Most punctures are found near whatever is up there that needs visiting.

  5. Freeze-thaw at the edges

    Ice builds at a perimeter or behind a parapet, lifts the membrane off its adhesion, and the next thaw opens a path for water. Ohio winters do this reliably to any edge that was not held properly.

A flat roof leak is diagnosed the same way as any other: from inside, from the deck where it can be reached, and from the roof, working uphill from the stain. On a membrane there is an extra step — the water travels sideways freely under the sheet, so the point where it appears inside can be a long way from the point where it entered.

That is why we do not quote a flat roof repair from a description of a ceiling stain, and why we will not seal a seam we have not found the end of.

If the roof is small and the membrane is at the end of its life, replacement is usually the better buy than a second repair on the same sheet. Common questions

What homeowners ask about flat roofs

Can you put shingles on a porch roof?

Below a certain pitch, no, and doing it anyway is how a porch leaks every year for a decade. Where the pitch is borderline we will tell you which side of the line it sits on and what the shingle manufacturer's own instructions say about it.

Which membrane lasts longest?

Installation quality moves the answer more than material choice does. A well-made EPDM roof with clean adhered seams outlasts a badly welded TPO roof every time. We specify the material and then we make the seams and check them.

Is ponding water normal?

A little, briefly, after a heavy rain, on an older roof with a settled deck. Standing water for days is not normal and it is not something to live with. Tapered insulation creates a fall where there is not one, and that is a designed fix rather than a repair.
