Slate roofing

Natural and synthetic slate, honestly compared

We install both. They are not the same product, they do not carry the same weight, they do not cost the same, and they do not look the same from a ladder.

Free estimate How matching works

Stone, split thin

Natural slate

Quarried rock, split into thin plates, punched with a nail hole and hung in overlapping courses. Nothing about it is manufactured except the cutting.

A slate roof is a hanging system, not a sealed one. Each slate is nailed near its head and covered by the courses above it, with a stated headlap so water has to travel up and over two layers before it reaches the deck. That headlap is the reason a slate roof works at all, and it is the first thing a cheap installation gives away.

Slate quality varies by quarry and by vein. Hard, dense slate survives central Ohio freeze-thaw for generations. Softer, more absorbent slate takes on water, freezes and delaminates — it splits along its own grain and sheds layers. On an old roof that shows as slates which have thickened, curled or spalled at the exposed edge, and it is the difference between a repair and a replacement.

What usually kills a good slate roof is not the slate. It is the fasteners, the flashings, and people walking on it. Nails fatigue and let go decades before the stone does, and valleys and chimneys in anything other than copper or a compatible metal fail while the field is still sound.

  • Quarried stone, split to thickness, hung in courses with a stated headlap.
  • Nailed near the head with a copper or hot-dipped fastener, never plain steel.
  • Hard slate survives Ohio freeze-thaw for generations; soft slate delaminates.
  • Fasteners and flashings fail before the stone does.
  • Individual slates can be replaced, which makes it a repairable roof.

Slate roofs meet chimneys constantly, and most leaks we find on older Columbus slate are at that junction rather than in the field. The masonry side is on the chimney page.

The alternative

Synthetic slate

A moulded composite made to read as slate. It is a good product and it is not stone, and we would rather say that up front than let you find out on a ladder.

Synthetic slate is formed from a composite or polymer material, cut or moulded into slate-shaped units and hung in courses with a headlap, much like natural slate. It is dramatically lighter, so it goes on roofs whose framing was never designed for stone. It is uniform, so the field reads even and there is no sorting on site, and it installs faster.

From the street on a steep roof at normal viewing distance, a good synthetic reads as slate. Up close it does not: the texture is moulded rather than split, the edge is even rather than feathered, the thickness is constant rather than varied, and the color is through-and-through rather than mineral. It also ages differently — some products lose their sheen and shift tone under UV, and the way they change is not the way stone changes.

None of that makes it the wrong choice. On a house whose structure cannot carry stone, or where natural slate is simply out of reach, synthetic gets you the profile, the shadow line and the steep-roof character without the weight or the cost. We just will not sell it to you as the same thing.

Side by side

Weight, structure, look and cost band

Four real differences. None of them is a reason to walk away from either product; all of them are reasons to choose with your eyes open.

Weight, and what has to carry it

Natural slate is heavy in a way that changes the project. It carries multiples of the dead load of a shingle roof, and the load rises again when the slate is wet and again under snow. Before we quote it on a house that never had it, the framing gets looked at: rafter size and spacing, ridge and bearing conditions, and what the decking is. Older slate houses were built with solid board decking and heavier rafters because they had to be. A house framed for shingles may need structural work, and that work goes on the proposal rather than being discovered on install day. Synthetic is a fraction of the weight, close enough to a heavy architectural shingle that most existing framing takes it without discussion.

Look

From the street both read as slate. From a ladder, only one is stone. Natural slate varies in thickness, edge and tone across a single course, and that variation is what a Tudor or a Georgian was drawn to have. Synthetic is even, which is a virtue on some houses and a tell on others.

Cost band

Stated as a band, never as a number: natural slate sits above synthetic slate, and both sit well above architectural shingles and above standing seam. Natural slate on a steep roof with a lot of hips, valleys and dormers sits above natural slate on a simple gable by a wide margin. We price your roof, not a category.

Service life, repair and Ohio weather

Good hard slate on a sound structure outlives almost everything else you can put on a house, and it is repairable one slate at a time for as long as the field holds. Synthetic has a shorter expected life and a manufacturer warranty rather than a century of precedent behind it. Freeze-thaw is the test natural slate either passes or fails depending on how absorbent the stone is; hail breaks slates rather than bruising them, and a broken slate is a visible, replaceable unit. Both shed snow fast on a steep pitch, so snow retention and the gutter below get designed rather than assumed.

The local stock

Upper Arlington Tudors, and the roofs under them

The real slate context in this metro is Upper Arlington. The Tudor Revival stock there was built with steep gables, half-timbering, prominent chimneys and, on a good number of houses, a slate field still doing its job a century later. That stock is why we keep slate as a working trade rather than a specialty we send out.

What usually arrives from that neighbourhood is not a new slate roof. It is an original field with a run of broken slates along one slope, a valley replaced in the wrong metal at some point, a chimney flashing re-caulked rather than re-cut, or a section where the nails have let go and the slates have slipped. All of that is repairable, and repairing it is usually the right answer.

The harder question is matching. Salvage and reclaimed slate is how a repair blends, and slate is sorted by thickness, size and tone — a new slate dropped into a weathered field reads as new for years. We bring the options to the house and show you the difference in your own daylight, the same way we do with shingles. That process is on the shingle matching page, and the neighbourhood itself is on roofing and exterior work in Upper Arlington.

  • Broken or slipped slates replaced individually, with slate bibs and a proper nail.
  • Valleys rebuilt in copper, with the field either side detailed into them.
  • Chimney flashings cut and counterflashed, not sealed over.
  • Salvage and reclaimed slate sorted on site for tone and thickness.
  • Framing and decking assessed before any new natural slate is quoted.
  • Headlap, exposure and nail specification written into the proposal.

Sometimes the honest recommendation is to leave an old slate field alone and fix the flashing. We would rather say that than sell a roof that did not need replacing.

Common questions

What homeowners ask about slate

Can my house take natural slate?

If it was built with slate, almost certainly. If it was framed for shingles, maybe — and the answer comes from looking at rafters, decking and bearing points rather than from a rule of thumb. Where the structure cannot carry it, synthetic slate or a heavy designer shingle gets you the profile without the load.

Is a slate roof worth repairing?

Usually, when the field is hard slate and the failures are localized: broken units, a bad valley, a chimney flashing, a run of slipped slates. When the slates themselves are delaminating across the roof, repair is a holding action and we will tell you that rather than take your money for it.

Will a repair be visible?

A new slate in a weathered field reads as new for a while, and anyone who says otherwise is selling you something. We sort salvage for tone and thickness, place new units where the eye forgives them, and show you the two side by side on your own roof before you decide.

A slate leak is usually a flashing leak. Our roofing repairs in Columbus cover valleys, chimneys and sidewalls, and the wider silo is on the Columbus roofing hub.
