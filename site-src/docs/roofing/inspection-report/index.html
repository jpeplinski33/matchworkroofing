Reports and condition letters

Dated roof inspection reports and condition letters

A written account of a roof's condition as observed on a specific day, with photographs, and with its limits stated on the face of the document.

The document

What this is

Three statements, and the whole document is built to support them.

It states observed condition. What we saw on the roof, from the ground and in the attic where it could be reached, in plain language, organised by slope. Not a prediction and not a rating.

It is dated. Every finding belongs to the day the inspection was performed. The date is on the document, and so is the expiry window that follows from it.

It is photographed. Findings carry photographs taken at the inspection, keyed to the slope and the detail they describe, so a reader who has never stood on the roof can see what the words point at.

Where repairs were performed by us, the report records what was repaired, with what material, and where. Those repairs carry a repair-only warranty on the terms in the proposal for that work, stated beside the finding they relate to rather than buried at the end.

The document is used most often three ways: a seller who wants a roof question answered before it becomes a negotiation, a buyer whose home inspection raised a roof remark and wants a roofing contractor's account of the same roof, and an agent who needs something in the file that says what was looked at and what was not. It is also useful to a homeowner with no transaction attached, as a dated, photographed baseline.

If what you want is the inspection rather than the document, that is on the roof inspection page, which sets out what we look at from the ground, on the roof and in the attic.

Contents

What the report contains

In this order, every time. Nothing is added or dropped to suit the reader.

Date, scope and method

The date it was performed, what was examined, how, and which vantage points were available. Where a slope could not be safely walked, that is written here.

The property, as identified by the client

The address as given to us by whoever engaged the inspection. We record it as provided; we do not verify a legal description or a parcel record.

Findings by slope and detail

The field, every flashing and penetration, valleys, eaves and rakes, ridge ventilation, gutters and their interface, and the attic side where it could be reached.

Photographs keyed to the findings

Taken at the inspection and labelled to the slope and detail they show, so the written finding and the image are read together.

Statement of observed condition

A plain-language summary of condition as observed on the date of inspection, each item bucketed: repair now, monitor, or no deficiency observed.

Repairs performed, where applicable

What was done, with what material, where, and on what date — with the repair-only warranty that attaches to it, stated beside it.

The not-a-warranty line

Stated explicitly, in its own paragraph: this document is not a warranty on the roof system. It is a record of what was observed on one day.

The expiry window

Printed on the document itself, beside the inspection date. After it, the report no longer describes the roof, because the roof has had weather since.

Exclusions and limitations

Concealed conditions, inaccessible areas, weather after the inspection date, and work performed by others. On the face of the document, not in a footnote.

Stated plainly

What the report does not say

  • No remaining life in years. No number of years appears in it, because nobody can honestly put one on a roof they inspected once.
  • It is not a warranty on the roof system, and it says so on its face.
  • Not a valuation, an appraisal, or an opinion about the property's worth.
  • No promise that a lender will accept it, that a buyer will, or that a property insurer will. That decision is theirs and not ours.
  • No opinion on anything concealed, anything inaccessible, or any work performed by someone else.
  • It does not override a home inspector's report. It is a roofing contractor's account of the same roof, from a different depth of examination.

What we warrant

Matchwork warrants the repairs we performed, on the terms in your proposal. A repair is not a warranty on the rest of the roof, the siding, the gutters or the masonry we did not touch, and we will never present it as one.

That callout appears on every repair page on this site and in the report itself wherever repairs are recorded, using the same sentence deliberately, so nothing has to be translated between the two documents.

A report that recorded a repair and then implied the whole roof had been warranted would be worth nothing to the person reading it in a closing file. The narrow version is the honest one, and it is the one that holds up.

Why it expires

The expiry window

Every roof inspection is a photograph in time, literally as well as figuratively. Between the day we walk the roof and the day somebody reads the report, that roof has had rain, freeze-thaw, wind, UV and possibly another storm.

So the document carries an expiry window, printed on the report itself rather than being something we mention afterwards. Anyone reading it can see on the face of it how old the observations are and how long they were considered current for.

We do not state that window here as a number of months, because it depends on the roof, the season it was inspected in, what was found, and whether the property is in an active transaction. It is set when the report is written, and it is written into the document.

If a report has expired and the question is still live, the answer is a new inspection and a new dated report, not an amendment to the old one.

  • The inspection date is printed on the document.
  • The expiry window is printed beside it, on the same face.
  • Both are visible without turning the page over.
  • After the window closes, the report no longer describes the roof.
  • A live question after expiry needs a new dated inspection.

The standard four

Exclusions, and why each one is there

These four appear on every report. They are not fine print; they describe what one inspection on one day cannot see.

Concealed conditions

Decking under a second roof layer, framing behind a finished attic surface, water inside a wall cavity and buried fasteners are not visible without opening the assembly. We do not guess at them and we do not report on them. Where we suspect one, the report says what we suspected and what would be needed to see it.

Inaccessible areas

A slope too steep or fragile to walk safely, a section behind a locked gate, an attic with no hatch or walkway, a roof under snow or standing water. These are named individually as areas not examined rather than being left out silently.

Future weather

The report describes what a roof was doing on the day it was inspected. It cannot account for the storm that arrives afterwards, the freeze-thaw that follows, or the wind event that lifts a tab which was seated and sealed that morning.

Work performed by others

We report on what we can see of someone else's repair and we do not warrant it. If a flashing has been sealed over where metal should have been cut in, the report describes it. It does not adopt it, guarantee it, or say how long it will last.

The format

What the document looks like

We do not publish a specimen of the report on this page. The format is walked through with you at the inspection itself, section by section, so you can see what is being recorded and ask about anything that is not.

It is then provided in writing, on paper and by email, with the photographs keyed to the findings, the date and the expiry window on the face of it, and the exclusions listed rather than footnoted.

If you want to know what will be in it before you book, the contents are listed above in full and the wording of every limiting statement is described on this page. Nothing in the document is a surprise relative to what you have read here.

For agents

How this fits a transaction

A roof remark in a home inspection is one of the more common things to slow a closing, usually because nobody can separate an item that has to be repaired before closing from an item that can be monitored for years.

That triage is what our Close-Ready program for realtors is built around. An agent sends the inspection report, we read it line by line, we visit, and we return a written scope separating legitimate roofing, siding, gutter and masonry items from monitor-only remarks from items that are not ours at all. Repairs get done and documented with dated photographs, and the completion letter goes into the buyer's file.

We never claim to override the home inspector's finding. We translate it into roofing terms and say what it means for the roof.

  • Pre-listing: an inspection and a dated report before the house goes on the market.
  • In contract: a report written to the transaction, with work scheduled around showings and the closing date.
  • Post-repair: the report records what was done, with what material, carrying the repair-only warranty beside it.
  • Expired report: a new dated inspection, not an amendment.

Timing language only, never a promise: we work to your closing date and schedule around showings. What the roof itself will do is described in the report and nowhere else.

Common questions

What people ask before they order a report

Will my lender or my buyer accept it?

We do not know and we will not say they will. Acceptance belongs to a lender, a buyer, a buyer's agent or a property insurer, and none of them is us. What we can say is what the document contains, how it is dated and what it excludes, so you can ask the right party the right question.

How long is the roof good for, according to the report?

The report does not answer that and no honest inspection can. It states what we observed on the date we observed it, and lists what needs repair now, what should be monitored and what showed no deficiency. A number of years is not in the document.

Can you write about work another roofer did?

We can describe what we can see of it, and we will. We cannot warrant it, adopt it, or say how long it will last, because we did not perform it and cannot see underneath it. That limitation is written in as the exclusion for work by others.

A report describes a roof; it does not repair one. If what you need is work done — a leak, a flashing failure, a valley or a chimney detail — that is on the roof leak and flashing repair page, the four-trade list is on exterior repairs in Columbus, and the wider silo on the Columbus roofing hub.
