Stone veneer

Natural and manufactured stone veneer

Veneer is a thin layer of stone hung on a wall, not a wall made of stone. That distinction is the whole job, and nearly every veneer failure we get called to is somebody who forgot it.

Material

Natural thin stone versus manufactured stone

Natural thin stone veneer is real stone sawn to a thin bed — limestone, sandstone, granite, quartzite, slate. Every piece varies in color, thickness and texture, so the wall has to be laid out and blended by eye as it goes up. It is heavier, it is a premium material in cost band, and it ages like the stone it is.

Manufactured stone veneer is cast Portland-based stone from a mold, colored with mineral oxide pigments through the body and on the face. It is lighter, more consistent in thickness, and a great deal easier to lay flat and level. It is also more porous than natural stone and more sensitive to water sitting against the back of it.

Both are veneers, and both need the same drainage assembly behind them. The choice is about look, weight and budget: natural stone on a New Albany entrance elevation reads differently from a cast ledger stone panel on a Dublin porch, and neither is wrong if it is detailed properly.

The assembly

Scratch coat, weep detail, flashing and the drainage gap

Four details, in order, from the wall outward. Skip any one and the veneer becomes a sponge held against your sheathing.

  1. Weather barrier and the drainage gap

    A water-resistive barrier over the sheathing, lapped shingle-fashion, then a drainage space behind the veneer so any water that gets through the stone has somewhere to go and a way down. Without the gap, water parks against the back of the stone and migrates into the wall.

  2. Through-wall flashing at the base

    Flashing that runs through the veneer and out past the face of the wall at the bottom of the assembly, and at every interruption — above windows, doors, porch roofs and any horizontal ledge. It catches what runs down inside the wall and sends it back outside.

  3. Weep detail

    Open weeps at the base course, at a spacing that lets the cavity drain. Mortar droppings bridging the cavity and blocking the weeps is the single most common defect on veneer work, because the droppings are invisible once the stone is set.

  4. Scratch coat and setting

    A scratch coat combed horizontally over lath gives the stone something mechanical to bond to. Then each piece is buttered and set, jointed, and tooled. On manufactured stone the layout is blended across the wall so the mold repeats are not visible.

Where it goes wrong

Veneer of convenience

The failure pattern we see most on Columbus porches and front elevations is veneer installed as a decorative skin with no cavity behind it: stone buttered directly to sheathing or to a single layer of felt, no through-wall flashing, no weeps, no gap, and the bottom course buried into a concrete stoop or the finished grade.

That assembly holds water. The veneer wicks moisture from the ground at the base, the cavity that does not exist cannot drain, and the water moves inward into the sheathing and framing. Externally it shows as efflorescence, dark damp patches at the bottom of the wall, spalled faces after a freeze, and mortar joints that crumble. Internally it shows as rot at the sill and framing behind the stone, which is where the real cost lives.

The other version of the same mistake is a veneer added over an existing wall without addressing what is behind it: a roof dumping onto the top of the new stone, a downspout discharging into the base course, or a porch roof meeting the veneer with no kickout. The stone is new, the detail is not, and the wall fails anyway.

What we do instead: assess the substrate first, build the drainage assembly, flash every horizontal transition, keep the base course clear of grade and concrete, and integrate whatever roof or gutter lands on or near the veneer. Where a porch roof meets a stone wall, that is a roof-to-wall flashing detail before it is a masonry one, and it is detailed the same way we detail a roof-to-stucco transition.

Repairs follow the same logic. A failing panel is not patched over; the stone comes off, the substrate and the flashing are assessed and corrected, and the veneer is rebuilt with a working cavity. Reclaiming and reusing the original stone is often possible on natural thin veneer and usually not on cast stone that has spalled.

Stone matching follows the same discipline as brick, described on brick matching and repair, and small localized wall repairs sit under the masonry section of our exterior repairs page.
