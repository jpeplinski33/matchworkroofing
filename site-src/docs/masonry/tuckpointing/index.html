Tuckpointing

Tuckpointing, mortar matching and what it saves

Repointing is the cheapest way to save an old brick wall and the fastest way to wreck it. The difference is the mortar you choose and how much of the old joint you take out.

Free estimate How matching works

Opening the joint

Raking out versus grinding, and why the depth matters

The joint has to be opened deep enough for new mortar to key in and no deeper than the brick can take. On most Columbus walls that is roughly two to two and a half times the joint width.

Raking uses hand tools or a small raking bar, following the joint. It is slow, quiet, and leaves the brick arris intact. Grinding with a diamond blade is faster but unforgiving: a blade that wanders a quarter inch cuts a permanent groove into the brick face, and it makes silica dust that has to be controlled.

We rake by hand wherever brick is soft, the wall is old or the joint is shallow. Grinders come out only on hard modern brick with deep, straight joints, and only with dust control.

Where the mortar is hard and the brick soft — the common pre-war failure — the old mortar comes out carefully, because it is stronger than the material around it and will take the brick face with it.

Repointing over a failing joint without opening it is a surface skin, and it peels out inside two winters.

Mortar matching

Profile, color and sand, in that order

Joint profile

Concave, V, weathered, grapevine, flush and raked all read differently and shed water differently. A concave joint tooled at the wrong radius changes the shadow line across an elevation. We match the profile already there, or tell you why it should change.

Color

Mortar color comes from the cement-to-lime ratio, any pigment, and above all the sand. A bag of gray mix does not match a 1960s buff joint, and white mix does not match a 1920s warm gray one. We mix to a sample from your wall.

Sand

Sand is most of the joint and most of the color. Grain size and mineral tint vary by quarry, so sand is matched before pigment is considered. A patch mixed with the wrong sand looks wrong however the color is tuned.

A test panel is mixed, laid on an inconspicuous part of the wall and left to cure first. Mortar lightens substantially as it dries, so a match judged wet is not a match.

The mistake we undo most

Why hard modern mortar destroys old brick

Two properties matter, and both are usually explained badly.

Compressive strength. A soft lime-based mortar is weaker than the brick it holds, deliberately. A wall moves with temperature and settlement and something has to give. When the mortar is weaker, the mortar cracks and the brick survives, and you repoint. When it is a hard Portland mix it does not give, so the brick does: faces pop off in flakes and arrises round away, and that damage cannot be repaired with mortar.

Vapor permeability. A soft mortar lets moisture evaporate back out through the joint. A dense modern mortar seals it, so water stays in the brick and leaves through the face instead. In a Columbus winter the brick is saturated when the temperature drops, the water expands as it freezes, and the surface spalls.

Freeze-thaw spalling looks like the brick face sanded off in patches, or shallow craters at the corners. It shows up a winter or two after a hard repoint, which is why the homeowner never connects it to the work that caused it.

Getting the mix right is the whole job, and nobody can see it once the wall is finished. That is why we photograph the mix and the test panel and put them in your proposal.
