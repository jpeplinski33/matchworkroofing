Stucco repair

Stucco repair and the roof-to-wall flashing that saves it

Stucco rarely fails because stucco is weak. It fails because water arrives from a roof above and the flashing at that junction was never detailed. Patch the wall, leave the transition, and you patch it again.

Roof to stucco

Kickout, step flashing and counterflashing into the stucco

Kickout flashing at the bottom of the run. Where the roof ends at the wall above a gutter, a kickout diverts the sheet of water into the gutter instead of down the face of the stucco. A missing kickout is the most destructive omission on a stucco house: concentrated runoff hits the same spot every rain, saturates the finish coat and works inward to sheathing and framing.

Step flashing up the slope. One piece per course of shingles, lapped so water sheds down and out, woven into the roof field. It cannot be replaced properly without lifting the shingles beside it.

Counterflashing into the stucco. Cut into a reglet or set behind the water-resistive barrier, lapped down over the step flashing, with the stucco terminated back to it on a proper sealant joint. Where counterflashing was caulked straight to the finish coat, the sealant fails within a few years and the wall takes water behind it.

We do both sides as one job: roof-side flashing under roof leak and flashing repair, stucco-side termination, reglet and patch here. Same logic applies where a roof meets stone veneer.

Diagnosis

Hairline cracking versus structural cracking

Almost every stucco wall has cracks. Which kind decides whether you are patching or rebuilding.

Hairline and craze cracking

Fine, shallow, often webbed, and confined to the finish coat. Caused by curing too fast, a rich mix or thin application. Cosmetically annoying, structurally irrelevant, repairable with a finish-coat refresh.

Shrinkage cracking at corners

Diagonal cracks from window and door corners are usually shrinkage plus missing or badly lapped control joints. Each one is a direct path for water into the frame, so they get opened and patched properly.

Structural cracking

Wide, stair-stepped, offset so one side sits proud of the other, or running continuously through a wall. That is movement in framing or foundation, and patching over it is pointless until the movement is addressed.

The system

Three-coat traditional stucco, in order

Traditional hard-coat stucco over a framed wall is three coats. The scratch coat goes onto lath and is scratched horizontally so the next coat keys in mechanically. The brown coat builds thickness, straightens the wall plane and cures slowly and damp. The finish coat carries texture and color.

Skip the brown coat and the finish coat is too thin to bridge movement, which is how a wall webs with cracks. Rush the cure between coats and shrinkage cracking appears at every corner.

Behind all three: a water-resistive barrier lapped shingle-fashion, flashing at every horizontal interruption, and a weep path at the base. Stucco is a reservoir cladding, designed to get wet and dry out, so drainage behind it matters more than thickness of it.

Blending

Patch blending and color matching

A patch is visible for two reasons: texture and color. Texture is matched by duplicating the application method — dash, smooth trowel, float, lace — and feathering the patch out so the boundary is gradual, not a hard rectangle.

Color is matched against the aged wall, not the original mix: sun shifts the pigment and runoff darkens the lower courses. We mix to a sample, lay a test patch and let it cure, because stucco lightens as it dries.

Where a patch will still read we say so, and offer the alternative: extend the repair to a natural break such as a control joint or a corner, so the boundary looks intentional. Matching follows the Matchwork method.

Synthetic stucco

EIFS repair is a different system, and it is not patched like stucco

EIFS — exterior insulation and finish system, often called synthetic stucco — looks like hard-coat stucco from the street and behaves nothing like it.

Traditional stucco is a thick cementitious skin over a drainage path. EIFS is a thin polymer finish coat over mesh, over a base coat, over insulation board fastened to the sheathing. No cement in the finish, no three-coat build, and in a barrier-type assembly no drainage cavity behind the foam at all. That is why EIFS repair is its own discipline, and why stucco patch technique applied to EIFS fails or telegraphs.

EIFS repair involves identifying whether the assembly is barrier-type or drained, cutting damaged finish and base coat back to sound material, replacing wet or compressed insulation board rather than coating over it, re-meshing with reinforcement lapped past the cut, and reapplying base and finish coats matched in texture and color. Terminations get resealed with compatible sealant.

Failures on Columbus EIFS walls are almost always at terminations rather than in the field: window and door surrounds, porch ledger intersections, the wall base at grade, and roof-to-wall transitions with no kickout or counterflashing. Water gets in there, the foam holds it against the sheathing, and framing is damaged long before the finish coat shows it.

Common questions

Stucco questions

How do I know if my wall is stucco or EIFS?

Tap it. Hard-coat stucco rings solid; EIFS sounds hollow and feels springy because foam sits behind the finish. A drill probe at a hidden spot settles it.

Can a crack just be filled?

A hairline crack, yes. Anything wider or offset gets opened, the substrate assessed and the coats rebuilt. Filling a moving crack with sealant gives you a shiny line that opens again.

Can stucco be repaired in cold weather?

Cementitious coats need warmth and time to cure, and freezing early ruins them. Some work can be protected; most waits for the season. We will tell you which.
