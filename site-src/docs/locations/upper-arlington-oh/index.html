Upper Arlington, Ohio

Upper Arlington's slate roofs, brick walls and chimneys

The oldest streets here were built in brick, stucco and slate, and the houses still standing on them were built to be repaired rather than re-skinned.

Free estimate Contact Matchwork

Housing stock

Tudors on the old streets, ranches on the newer ones

Old Arlington's oldest streets date to the 1920s through the 1940s: brick and stucco Tudor Revivals and center-hall colonials, many with steep multi-plane rooflines and, on a handful of the oldest homes near the Scioto River, original slate. The 1950s and 1960s filled in the rest of the city with ranches and split-levels.

Tudor Revival

Brick, stucco and dark half-timber trim on steep pitches.

Mid-century ranches

Brick and brick-and-siding homes on lower-pitch, simpler shapes.

Original slate

Still on some river-side houses, where the decking was sized for it.

Four trades

Roofing, siding, gutters and masonry in Upper Arlington

All four trades here meet at one detail: where a steep roof runs into a brick wall.

Roofing: slate, natural and synthetic

Slipped or cracked slates get replaced in kind, with the headlap kept intact. Where the structure will not carry natural slate's weight, synthetic slate reads close from the street at a lower weight and a lower cost band. Slate and steep-pitch scope: the roofing trade page.

Siding: stucco gables, painted wood

Tudor gables carry stucco and half-timber; the walls below are usually painted wood that fails at the bottom course first. Ranches carry vinyl or aluminum, and the trim behind it rots before the siding does. Wall materials and trim: the siding trade page.

Gutters: steep roofs shed fast

A steep Tudor plane dumps water hard, and original steel gutters here have rusted through at the seams. We hang half-round and copper on the older houses, seamless aluminum on the ranches. Profiles and drainage: the gutter trade page.

Masonry: brick, mortar and chimneys

This is the trade Upper Arlington asks for most. We match brick and mortar to a 1920s wall, tuckpoint open joints, and rebuild chimneys with new crowns and counterflashing cut into the masonry. Mortar matching and rebuilds: the masonry trade page.

Color matching

Brick, stucco and half-timber do not move

Those three materials are fixed once they are on the house, which puts the matching work on the new slate, the new mortar and the new gutter. We compare sample boards and mortar pats against the actual wall in daylight before ordering. The approach across four trades: the Matchwork Method.

Before you order

No citywide design-review board here

Upper Arlington does not run a citywide design-review board the way New Albany or Muirfield Village do, and we will not pretend one exists. Permits still apply to the work, and a few individual streets carry their own covenants, worth reading before a color is picked.

What governs the match on most of these blocks is the block itself. A wrong-toned roof or a bright new mortar joint stands out on a street of consistent brick and stucco with no committee needed to notice it.

Storm exposure

Hail on slate, limbs in the canopy, ice in the joints

The same seasonal systems that cross Dublin and Powell reach Upper Arlington with hail and straight-line wind. Mature canopy along the Scioto adds limb-strike exposure open subdivisions see less of, and a limb landing on slate breaks slates rather than denting shingles.

The slow damage is freeze-thaw: water into an open joint, ice, and a face spalls off the brick — the failure mode behind most masonry work on these streets.

Repairs

Repair first, and say so in writing

Slate roofs can often be repaired course by course for decades; a chimney usually needs a crown, a cap or a rebuild, not a new roof attached to it. We say which, in writing. Slate, flashing and chimney repair: the exterior repairs page.

What we warrant

Matchwork warrants the repairs we performed, on the terms in your proposal. A repair is not a warranty on the rest of the roof, the siding, the gutters or the masonry we did not touch, and we will never present it as one.

Realtors

Older houses draw closer inspections

A buyer on a 1920s house asks different questions than on a new build, and slate, chimney and gutter remarks are common ones. We photograph observed condition, do the work the report actually calls for, and let a closing date set the sequence. The Close-Ready program: the realtor page.
