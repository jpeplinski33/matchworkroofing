Storm diagnostics

Hail bruising vs. blistering: how to tell them apart

Not every mark on a shingle came from a storm. Blisters and old manufacturing flaws can look like hail from a distance, and telling them apart correctly matters — both for an honest inspection and for a claim that holds up.

Genuine hail

What an actual hail bruise looks and feels like

A hailstone strikes a shingle with real kinetic force, and that force has to go somewhere. On an asphalt shingle, it typically fractures the fiberglass mat underneath the surface without necessarily tearing the shingle open. Press a finger into a genuine hail hit and it usually gives slightly, a soft or spongy feel where the mat beneath has cracked, unlike the surrounding shingle, which stays firm. A few other things tend to travel with real hail:

  • Granules driven into the asphalt at the point of impact rather than missing from it, sometimes with a visible dark divot at the center.
  • A random but wind-correlated scatter across a slope, rather than one isolated spot with nothing else nearby.
  • Matching dents on soft metal in the same area — roof vents, gutters, downspouts, or an outdoor air-conditioner's fins — since hail marks anything soft it hits, not just shingles.

Direction is part of the evidence too. A storm that tracked from the southwest will leave its work on the southwest-facing slopes and on the metal that faced it, and will leave a sheltered north slope comparatively clean. Marks that appear evenly on every face of the house, including the ones under an overhang or behind a chimney, are worth a second look before anybody calls them hail.

Not hail

What a blister looks like instead

A blister forms when a small pocket of trapped gas or moisture inside the shingle expands, usually from heat, and pushes the surface up into a hollow dome from underneath. It's a different mechanism entirely from an impact, and it tends to show different physical evidence:

  • A raised, hollow bump with a firm, defined rim, rather than a soft, sunken divot.
  • The fiberglass mat underneath stays intact and rigid — there's no fracture to find when you check it.
  • Blisters often show up on slopes with the least wind exposure just as readily as the most exposed ones, since heat and trapped moisture don't correlate with storm direction the way an impact does.
  • They can appear scattered across an entire roof, sometimes on shingles installed at the same time from the same production run, rather than being clustered where a storm actually tracked.

A blister that has popped leaves its own signature: a small crater with a raised, brittle rim and granules missing from the centre rather than crushed into it. That is the version most often mistaken for a hail hit from the ground, because both leave a dark spot on a shingle. From on top of the roof, the rim tells you which one you have.

How to tell

How an inspector separates the two on the roof

The check is physical, not visual guesswork from the ground. An inspector marks off a test square on each slope, counts every mark inside it, and evaluates each one individually: press it, check for mat fracture underneath, and note whether it lines up with dents on nearby soft metal and with the wind direction the storm actually came from. A pattern of soft, granule-embedded marks that correlates with damaged gutters and vents reads as hail. A scatter of hard-rimmed domes with no soft-metal correlation, or a defect repeating uniformly across shingles from the same batch, reads as something other than storm damage.

Everything found inside that square gets photographed in place, with the slope and the direction it faces recorded against it, so the mark can be looked at again later by someone who wasn't standing there. That documentation is what turns an opinion into a finding, and it is the part that gets skipped when a roof is assessed from a ladder or from a drone image alone. What a roof inspection looks at goes through the whole visit, and a dated roof inspection report is what that visit produces when a house is being sold.

This distinction isn't about winning an argument with a carrier — it's about giving an honest, physically documented answer to a fair question. Sometimes a roof genuinely has both storm damage and unrelated blistering on it, and a good inspection says so plainly rather than rounding everything up to one conclusion.

Other marks

What else on a shingle gets called hail

Hail is not the only thing that leaves a mark, and a roof that has been up for a while usually carries several kinds at once. Four that come up constantly on central Ohio houses:

  • Mechanical damage. A ladder leaned against a slope, a boot on a hot summer shingle, or a satellite or solar mounting point walked on during service leaves a scuff, a crack or a crushed tab that has nothing to do with weather.
  • Wind creasing. A shingle lifted and flexed in straight-line wind develops a horizontal crease, often with a broken sealant strip. That is wind, and it is assessed differently from an impact.
  • Normal ageing. Granule loss, curling tabs and a dull, blotchy surface across a sun-facing slope are wear. What granule loss in your gutters is telling you covers how that presents.
  • Tar spots and sealant blobs. Dark, shiny patches left by a previous repair read as a mark from the ground and as nothing at all from on top of the roof, once you can see they're sitting on the surface rather than in it.

None of that is a reason to dismiss a roof. It's a reason to be specific about what each mark is, because a scope built on the wrong label fixes the wrong thing.

Why it matters

Why the difference matters for a claim

A functional hail bruise is the kind of damage a wind and hail policy is written to cover; a manufacturing blister generally isn't, and the two are worth separating carefully before anything is filed. Where things go sideways is when an inspection blurs them together in either direction — calling ordinary blistering storm damage, or waving off a real hail hit because a few blisters happen to be on the same roof. A documented, test-square inspection gives you and your adjuster the same physical facts to work from, which is the whole point of doing one before a claim is filed rather than after.

It also matters for the shingle itself. Blistering that's been there for years, quietly aging alongside the rest of the roof, isn't something a storm claim was ever meant to address, and treating it that way tends to slow a claim down rather than help it. A clear inspection separates the two questions from the start: is there storm damage here, and separately, is this roof also just getting old. Both can be true on the same roof at the same time, and neither answer changes the other.

That second question is the one most homeowners actually want answered, and it does not need a storm to be worth asking. A roof can be worn out on a south slope, sound on a north one, and never have been hit by anything.
