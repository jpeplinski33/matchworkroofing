Roof diagnostics

What granule loss in your gutters is actually telling you

A little colored grit at the bottom of a downspout after a storm is normal. A drift of it, or a shingle gone shiny and dark in patches, is a different conversation. Here is how to tell which one you're looking at.

The basics

Why a shingle is covered in granules in the first place

An asphalt shingle is built in layers: a fiberglass mat in the middle, a waterproof asphalt coating around it, and a top layer of small, ceramic-coated stone granules pressed into that coating. The granules aren't decorative. They shield the asphalt underneath from direct sun, add resistance to fire and wind-driven debris, and absorb the impact of hail and foot traffic so the waterproof layer underneath doesn't have to take it directly. Once a patch of granules is gone, the asphalt under that patch is exposed to UV on its own, and it ages a lot faster than the shingle around it.

What's normal

Normal shedding on a roof that's still new

Every new architectural shingle sheds a certain amount of loose granule in its first few real rains. Some of it never bonded tightly during manufacturing, and it washes free the first time water moves across the field in volume. If your roof went on this year or last, and you're finding a light, even dusting of granules in the gutter after a storm, that's the roof settling in, not the roof failing. A few signs that what you're seeing is ordinary:

  • The color and texture of the shingles look the same across every slope, not just the ones that catch the most sun.
  • The gutter sediment is a thin layer you could brush off, not a sludge you have to scoop out.
  • There's no shiny or dark patch showing through anywhere on the roof itself.
  • The amount coming off is tapering with each rain rather than staying steady or increasing.

What isn't

What real granule loss looks like further along

A roof that's been on for a decade or more sheds differently. Instead of loose surface grit, granules start pulling away from spots where the asphalt coating itself is breaking down underneath them — usually the south- and west-facing slopes first, since those take the most direct sun over the life of the roof. Look for:

  • Bald patches where you can see bare, dark asphalt or a faint sheen of exposed fiberglass mat.
  • A noticeably heavier buildup of granules in the gutter troughs, closer to sand than dust.
  • Shingle edges that have started to curl, cup, or crack at the tab.
  • Color that's gone uneven — one or two slopes visibly duller or blotchier than the rest of the roof.

None of this is unusual for a shingle that's simply reached the end of its service life — it's wear, not storm damage, and it isn't something that runs through an insurance claim. It's a maintenance and replacement conversation, and it's worth having before a bald patch turns into a leak.

Why it's uneven

Why one slope goes bald before the others

Sun exposure isn't the same on every side of a house. A south-facing slope can run noticeably hotter through a Central Ohio summer than a north-facing one on the same roof, and that heat is what breaks the asphalt coating down over time. It's normal for one or two slopes to show real wear years before the rest of the roof does. That's also why a single bald patch usually isn't a good candidate for a spot repair — if part of a slope has aged out, the rest of that slope tends to be close behind it, even where it still looks fine from the ground.

Two other things drive the same uneven pattern around here. An attic that runs hot in summer bakes the decking from underneath and cooks the asphalt from both sides at once, which is why the ventilation gets measured and written into a proposal rather than assumed. And a roof-to-wall transition, a chimney or a dormer that shades part of a slope will leave that part looking newer than the field around it, which is a difference in exposure rather than a difference in the shingle.

Wear or weather

Granule loss is not the same thing as hail damage

The two get confused because both involve granules that are no longer where they started. The mechanism is different, and so is what you should do about it.

Wear lets go of granules from the surface outward, over a whole slope, gradually, on the faces that get the most sun. An impact drives granules into the asphalt at a single point, fractures the mat underneath it, and leaves a soft spot you can feel with a fingertip. A hail-damaged slope usually has scattered marks that line up with dents on soft metal nearby — vents, gutters, downspouts — because a hailstone marks anything soft it hits, not only shingles. A worn slope has none of that: no soft spots, no metal correlation, just a surface going bald in the sun.

Getting that distinction right matters before anything is filed, and it is a physical check rather than an opinion from the ground. How hail bruising and blistering are told apart goes through the checks one at a time, and how a storm inspection and a claim run explains what happens if the answer is genuinely weather.

Next steps

What to do if you're seeing bald spots

Start on the ground. Check the gutters after the next real rain, and look at each slope from the yard in good daylight rather than straight overhead sun, which hides detail with glare. If you're seeing the signs above — heavier sediment, dark patches, curling edges — it's worth having someone measure the roof and look at it up close before deciding what comes next. That's a maintenance and replacement conversation on its own, separate from anything storm- or insurance-related.

If you want to send photographs before anyone visits, three are more useful than thirty: the gutter trough with the sediment in it, the slope that sediment came off, shot from the yard at an angle that shows the field, and a close-up of any bald patch you can reach safely from a ladder without getting on the roof. Say which side of the house each one is on. Don't climb onto a pitched roof to get a better angle — that is the part of this that gets people hurt, and it never tells us anything we can't find from on top of it ourselves.

Where granule loss is one symptom among several — a leak, a lifted flashing, a soft spot around a pipe boot — the roof needs looking at as a system rather than a surface.
