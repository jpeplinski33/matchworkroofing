Half-round and copper

Why this profile, why this metal

A half-round reads as part of the house; a K-style reads as an appliance

K-style is a flat-backed box with a decorative front, and it suits most suburban elevations. Half-round is a semicircular trough sitting in round hangers, with the profile visible from the street. On a Tudor, a Georgian brick, a slate-roofed colonial or a cedar-shake house, the round line follows the architecture instead of interrupting it.

Copper is chosen for two reasons. It needs no paint, so there is no finish to fail on a south or west elevation taking full afternoon sun. And it is thick, workable metal that can be soldered rather than sealed — the difference between a joint that is continuous metal and a joint that is a bead of caulk waiting to shrink.

Copper is a premium material and its cost band sits well above painted aluminum. On the right house that is easy to justify. On the wrong one it is money spent on a detail nobody will see, and we will tell you which one you have. The rest of the range is on the gutter installation hub.

Half-round is also made in painted aluminum where the profile matters and the metal budget does not. Small gutter and eave fixes go through the gutter section of our exterior repairs page.

How it is hung

Round hangers, straps and soldered joints

Round hangers and straps

A half-round sits in a round hanger that wraps the trough, or hangs from a strap fastened to the fascia. Both must land in solid wood. On older houses we open the fascia first, because a strap into soft lumber pulls out under a wet winter run.

Soldered joints

Corners, outlets, end caps and the seams between lengths are cleaned, fluxed and soldered so the joint becomes continuous metal. Slower than sealant, and the reason a copper system outlasts the roof it drains.

Pitch and outlets

A round trough shows pitch errors honestly, because standing water sits visible in the bottom of the run. Outlets are sized to the roof area feeding them, and the downspout follows in copper or in a painted metal chosen at the house.

Over time

Patina is the finish doing its job

New copper is bright and orange-pink, and it will not stay that way. It darkens to a warm brown through the first seasons, then moves toward gray-green as the surface oxidizes. Exposure sets the rate: a south-facing eave in full sun moves faster than one shaded by maples, and runoff marks the light surface below the gutter first.

  • Bright mill copper at installation, solder lines visible.
  • Warm brown through the first few years of weather.
  • Gray-green over the long run, uneven where shelter and sun differ.
  • Streaking on brick, limestone sills and light trim below the eave. Normal, and we point it out before you decide.

Want the brown or green on day one? Copper can be ordered pre-patinated. Want no streaking on a light elevation? Half-round in painted aluminum is the honest alternative, and we will say so.

Where the system belongs: paired with natural slate or a heavy slate-profile roof, with cedar shake, and with brick and stone elevations that carry real cornice detail. On those houses the gutter is part of the composition.

In central Ohio that means the estate-scale brick houses of New Albany, where Georgian proportions and deep overhangs suit a round trough, and the older slate and brick stock of Upper Arlington, where Tudor eaves and heavy cornice lines are why half-round was specified in the first place.

If your roof is a standard architectural shingle on a builder-grade elevation, copper is likely more house than the gutter needs. We would rather say that than sell it.
